How to Keep a Cat Happy Indoors: A Complete Guide

November 21, 2026 • 9 min read • PawSmartCentral

An indoor cat does not need a garden to be happy. It needs a home that works like a cat's world: somewhere to hunt, somewhere to climb, somewhere to hide, something to scratch, and someone to share the day with. A cat kept indoors is safer from traffic, fights, and many diseases, and indoor cats often live longer than cats that roam freely. But safety alone does not make a cat happy. A cat left alone in a flat with a food bowl, a litter tray, and nothing to do will get bored — and a bored cat finds its own entertainment, usually in the curtains, the sofa, or the owner's ankles at three in the morning. This guide explains how to keep a cat happy indoors: daily play that copies hunting, vertical space and hiding places, scratching and litter routines that prevent problems, feeding that keeps the mind busy, a calm daily rhythm, and the warning signs that mean something needs to change. It is the eighth and final guide in this series on everyday pet care, and it draws on the others where a topic needs more detail.

Why Indoor Cats Need More Than Food and a Sofa

Cats are hunters, climbers, and watchers, even when they live in a small flat. In the wild, a cat's day is a cycle of hunting, eating, grooming, and sleeping, repeated several times. An indoor cat has the same instincts, but no mice to chase and no trees to climb. If nothing in the home answers those instincts, the energy has nowhere to go. That is when owners see the problems people blame on a "naughty" cat: scratching the furniture, knocking things off shelves, yowling at night, overeating, or sleeping all day and then racing around the house at midnight. None of those behaviours means the cat is bad. They mean the home is not giving the cat enough to do.

The fix is not expensive, and it is not one big purchase. It is a set of small, everyday habits: play that lets the cat hunt, furniture arranged so the cat can go up as well as across, scratching posts in the places the cat actually uses, food served in a way that takes effort, and a routine the cat can rely on. Think of the home from the cat's height. A cat experiences a room at floor level and at shelf level, through smells and sounds we barely notice, and through windows that are television screens for a cat. A room that looks tidy and calm to a person can look empty and dull to a cat. The rest of this guide walks through the home the way a cat experiences it, room by room and habit by habit.

Play Every Day: Let the Cat Hunt

Play is the single most important thing you can do for an indoor cat, and it is also the most often skipped. Aim for two or three short sessions a day, of about 10 to 15 minutes each. Short and frequent fits how cats hunt: a burst of stalking, chasing, and pouncing, then rest. One long session once a week does not count. The cat needs the chance to hunt every day, ideally at roughly the same times, so that play becomes part of the daily rhythm.

Use toys that move like prey. A wand toy with a feather or a small fabric mouse on a string, dragged along the floor, flicked around a corner, or dangled just out of reach, will get almost any cat moving. Let the toy hide behind furniture, pause, and dart away, the way a real animal would. Vary the movement: some cats love a toy that scurries along the ground, others prefer one that flutters in the air. Laser pointers can be fun for a few minutes, but never use one as the only toy, because the cat can never catch the dot, and a hunt that never ends in a catch is frustrating. If you do use one, finish by pointing it at a real toy or a treat the cat can actually catch and eat.

Always end a play session with a catch. Let the cat grab the toy, hold it, and "kill" it for a moment, then offer a small meal or a treat. That sequence — hunt, catch, eat, groom, sleep — is the pattern a cat's body expects, and finishing with food helps the cat settle. Put the wand toys away after play, in a drawer or a high shelf. A string left lying around is a danger if a cat chews and swallows it, and a toy that is always available stops being interesting. Rotate the other toys instead: keep a few out, and swap them every few days so that an old toy feels new again. A kitten will want more play than an adult, and its sessions should be shorter and gentler. Our guide to the best cat food for kittens covers feeding in the first year, and the same idea applies to play: a kitten's needs change fast as it grows, so watch the kitten and adjust. If you also have a dog, keep the cat's play separate from the dog's play at first — our guide to the best dog food for puppies is a reminder that a growing dog and a cat have very different needs, and a boisterous puppy can frighten a cat that is trying to play.

Solo play matters too. A cat left alone during the working day should have things to do without you: a crumpled paper ball, a cardboard box with holes cut in it, a treat ball that rolls food out slowly, or a toy that moves on its own for a few minutes at a time. Leave a radio or a calm television programme on quietly if it seems to help, but do not rely on background noise alone. The best solo enrichment is a window with a view of birds, a shelf to sit on, and a puzzle feeder that makes the cat work for its food. Change the solo toys regularly, and check them for damage — a toy with loose string, stuffing coming out, or a broken part should be thrown away.

Vertical Space, Windows, and Hiding Places

Cats feel safe when they can get up high and look down. A cat tree, a sturdy shelf, or a cleared top of a wardrobe gives the cat a lookout post, a place to rest undisturbed, and an escape route when the house gets noisy. You do not need to buy an expensive cat tree to start: a strong shelf fixed firmly to the wall, a step up to the top of a bookcase, or a window perch will do. What matters is that the route up is safe, the perch itself is stable, and the cat can get down again without jumping from a height that frightens it. In a small home, vertical space is the cheapest way to double the cat's territory. Use the walls.

Windows are a cat's television. A perch or a wide windowsill where the cat can sit and watch birds, leaves, and people passing is worth more than most toys. Make the window safe: keep it closed or fitted with a secure screen, never leave a window open wide enough for a cat to slip through or fall from, and be careful with windows that open at the top, where a cat can get stuck trying to squeeze through. A bird feeder outside the window, placed where the cat can watch but cannot reach, gives the cat something to follow all day. If the view is dull, a fish tank with a secure lid, or a short video made for cats, can help — but nothing replaces the real movement outside a window.

Hiding places are just as important as high places. Every cat needs a spot where it can go and not be disturbed: a covered bed, a cardboard box with a hole cut in the side, a quiet corner under a bed, or an igloo bed in a room nobody walks through much. A cat that is hiding is not necessarily unhappy — cats hide to rest, to feel safe, and to get away from noise. The problem is only when a cat hides far more than usual, which is covered in the warning-signs section below. Never drag a cat out of its hiding place, and teach children and visitors to leave a hiding cat alone. In a home with more than one cat, each cat needs its own hiding place, and its own high place, so that no cat has to compete for safety. The same rule applies to the litter tray and the food bowl: one per cat, plus one extra, spread around the home.

Scratching, Litter, and the Basics Done Well

Scratching is normal. A cat scratches to keep its claws in shape, to stretch its shoulders and back, and to leave scent and visual marks that say this place is mine. The question is never how to stop scratching. It is where the cat scratches. Give the cat a tall, sturdy scratching post that does not wobble, tall enough for the cat to stretch fully, placed near where the cat sleeps and near the furniture it already scratches. Offer both a vertical post and a flat or angled scratcher if you can, and try different materials — sisal rope, carpet, and cardboard all suit different cats. Reward the cat with a treat or praise when it uses the post, and keep the post in place even after the cat is using it well. Moving it to a corner out of sight is the fastest way to send the cat back to the sofa.

If the cat is already scratching furniture, cover the scratched corner temporarily with a throw or a protective cover, put the post right beside it, and trim the cat's claws regularly, taking care not to cut too short. Never punish scratching, and never have a cat declawed: declawing removes the last bone of each toe, it is painful, and it can leave a cat in lasting pain and more likely to bite. A cat that suddenly scratches much more than usual, or scratches one spot until it is sore, should be seen by a vet, because stress, skin problems, and pain can all show up as scratching.

The litter tray is the other basic that decides whether an indoor cat is content. Keep the tray in a quiet, accessible place, away from the food and water bowls, and away from a washing machine or another noisy appliance. Scoop it at least once a day, and change the litter fully on a regular schedule. Many cats refuse a dirty tray and will go elsewhere, and once a cat starts going elsewhere, the habit is hard to break. A good rule is one tray per cat, plus one extra, in different parts of the home, especially in a larger home or a home with more than one floor. If you change the type of litter, change it gradually, mixing the new litter into the old over several days. A cat that suddenly stops using the tray, strains, cries, or passes very little urine needs a vet urgently, because urinary problems in cats can become serious quickly.

Feeding: Make Meals Work for the Cat

An indoor cat that eats from a bowl in two minutes and then has nothing to do will often eat too much and move too little. Indoor cats are prone to weight gain because they burn fewer calories than a cat that roams, and extra weight brings its own problems, from stiff joints to diabetes risk. The answer is not to feed less and leave the cat hungry. It is to feed the right amount for an indoor cat, and to make meals take effort. Our guide to the best cat food for indoor cats explains how indoor formulas and portion sizes differ, and how to judge body condition by feeling the ribs and looking for a waist.

Scatter feeding and puzzle feeders turn a meal into a hunt. Scatter a portion of dry food across a clean floor or a mat so the cat has to search for it, or use a puzzle feeder — a ball with holes, a tray with compartments, or a homemade feeder from a cardboard tube with holes — that releases a few pieces at a time. Start easy, so the cat learns that the feeder gives food, and make it harder slowly. Feed measured meals rather than leaving a full bowl down all day, unless your vet has advised otherwise, and count treats in the daily amount. Fresh water matters as much as food: keep water in a clean bowl away from the food bowl, which many cats prefer, or try a cat water fountain, since many cats drink more from moving water. Wash the bowl daily. A cat that drinks far more or far less than usual, or whose appetite changes suddenly, should be seen by a vet.

Food sensitivities can also unsettle an indoor cat. A cat that scratches, over-grooms, vomits, or has soft stools after eating may be reacting to something in its food, and the label-reading habits in our guide to choosing food for dogs with allergies are a useful model for reading a cat food label carefully, even though that guide is written for dogs. Do not guess at an elimination diet or switch foods repeatedly without veterinary advice: a vet can advise whether a food trial is needed, and which food to try. Keep feeding times steady. A cat that knows when meals come is a calmer cat than one that never knows when the bowl will appear.

Routine, Company, and a Calm Home

Cats like routine more than owners often realise. Feed, play, and settle at roughly the same times each day, and the cat will learn the pattern and relax into it. A predictable day is especially important for a cat that is anxious, newly arrived, or sharing the home with another pet. Keep the home calm at the cat's level: avoid sudden loud noises near the cat's resting places, give the cat warning before a noisy appliance starts, and keep visitors from cornering the cat. Grooming is part of the routine too: brush a long-haired cat daily, and a short-haired cat a few times a week, to reduce hairballs and to give the cat calm, regular contact. Check ears, teeth, and claws during grooming, and treat grooming as a quiet time together rather than a chore to rush.

Company matters, but it must be on the cat's terms. Spend time in the same room as the cat, talk quietly, and let the cat come to you. Many cats enjoy sitting near their owner without being picked up, and a cat that walks away from a cuddle is saying no — let it go. If you are out for long hours, a second cat can be good company, but only if the introduction is done slowly, over days or weeks, with separate rooms, scent swapping, and short supervised meetings. Two cats that are forced together too fast may never settle. A single cat that has company from its owner, play every day, and a home full of things to do can be perfectly happy alone. Watch the cat: a cat that greets you, plays, eats well, and sleeps in the open is content. A cat that hides all day, even when the house is quiet, may be lonely, bored, or unwell — and the cause is worth finding.

Older cats need the same routine, with gentler adjustments. An older indoor cat may climb less, play less, and sleep more, and it may need lower perches, a warmer bed, easier access to the litter tray, and more frequent vet checks. Our senior cat care checklist covers food, litter tray, and vet visits for an older cat in detail. If you share the home with an older dog as well, remember that the dog's pace and the cat's pace are different: our guide to the best dog food for senior dogs explains the older dog's needs, and keeping the cat's high places and hiding spots dog-free helps both animals relax.

Safe Adventures: Balconies, Harnesses, and Fresh Air

An indoor cat can still enjoy fresh air safely. A balcony fitted with a secure cat net, checked regularly for gaps, gives the cat sun, breeze, and birdwatching without the dangers of roaming. Never leave a cat unsupervised on an open balcony, and never rely on a cat's balance: cats do fall from balconies, and the fall can cause serious injury. A catio — a secure outdoor enclosure attached to a window or a door — is a larger version of the same idea, and even a small one gives the cat grass to sniff, sun to lie in, and new smells to explore.

Harness training is another option, and it suits some cats well. Use a harness made for cats, never a collar and lead, and introduce it indoors first: let the cat wear the harness for a few minutes at a time, with treats, until it walks normally in it. Then practise in a quiet, enclosed garden, keeping the lead short and letting the cat choose the direction. Some cats take to a harness in days; others never accept one, and forcing it will only frighten the cat. A cat that panics in a harness should be brought straight back inside. Never tie a cat up outside unattended, and never take a cat to a busy street, a dog-heavy park, or anywhere it cannot retreat. The point of safe adventures is new smells and gentle stimulation, not a walk. If the cat is happy watching from a window, that is enough.

Vet care and prevention still matter for a cat that never goes out. Indoor cats still need vaccinations, flea and worm treatment as advised by the vet, dental checks, and weight checks. Kittens need their first vet visits and vaccinations on schedule, and the principle is the same as for a young dog: our puppy vaccination schedule post is written for dogs, but the habit it teaches — ask the vet for the schedule, keep it, and do not miss a visit — applies to kittens and cats too. Ask your vet what your indoor cat needs, and keep a note of the dates. An indoor cat that is neutered, vaccinated, and checked regularly is a cat whose health problems are usually caught early, when they are easiest to treat.

Signs of a Happy Cat — and Signs Something Is Wrong

A happy indoor cat is easy to recognise once you know what to look for. It plays in bursts, eats with appetite, grooms itself calmly, sleeps in the open in a relaxed position, and greets you or comes to sit near you. It purrs, kneads, slow-blinks at you, and shows its belly — though a belly shown is an invitation to look, not always an invitation to touch. It uses the litter tray reliably, scratches its post, and explores the home with curiosity rather than fear. No cat is playful every hour of the day. Cats sleep for much of the day, and a cat that sleeps a lot but is alert and active when awake is usually fine.

Unhappiness and stress look different. Watch for hiding much more than usual, eating much less or much more, over-grooming until the fur thins or the skin is sore, spraying or toileting outside the tray, sudden aggression, or a cat that stops playing entirely. These signs can mean boredom, stress, pain, or illness, and the first step is always a vet visit, because a health problem must be ruled out before a behaviour problem is assumed. If the vet finds no health problem, look at the home with fresh eyes: more play, more hiding places, a cleaner litter tray, a quieter routine, and less competition with other pets often help. A change in the home — a move, a new baby, a new pet, building work — can unsettle a cat for weeks, and patience, routine, and a safe retreat help more than any single product. Do not use punishment for any of these behaviours: it increases stress, damages trust, and does not teach the cat what to do instead.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Skipping daily play — a cat that never gets to hunt will find its own outlets, usually the furniture, the curtains, or your feet.
  • Punishing scratching or spraying — both are normal cat communication and stress signals. Give the cat a post, a tray, and a reason to feel safe instead.
  • Leaving one litter tray for several cats — one tray per cat, plus one extra, in different places, prevents most toileting problems.
  • Feeding from a full bowl all day — measure meals, use puzzle feeders, and watch the waistline, especially for an indoor cat that moves less.
  • Forcing company — a cat that walks away from a cuddle, a harness, or a new pet is saying no. Slow down and let the cat choose.
  • Leaving windows and balconies unsecured — a cat can fall or slip through a gap that looks far too small. Screens and nets must be checked regularly.
  • Ignoring a change in behaviour — a cat that hides, stops eating, or over-grooms needs a vet visit first, not a new toy.
  • Changing food, litter, or routine all at once — cats cope badly with sudden change. Introduce one change at a time, gradually, and watch the response.

The Bottom Line

Keeping a cat happy indoors comes down to five daily habits: play that lets the cat hunt, vertical space and hiding places that let it feel safe, a scratching post and a clean litter tray in the right places, feeding that takes effort and keeps weight in check, and a calm routine with company on the cat's terms. Add safe fresh air where you can — a screened window, a netted balcony, or harness time for a cat that accepts it — and keep vet care up to date even though the cat never roams. Start with one change this week: two play sessions a day, or a shelf by the window, or a puzzle feeder at mealtimes. Watch the cat for a week, and let its behaviour tell you what to add next. Frequently asked questions

Do indoor cats need to go outside to be happy?

No. An indoor cat can be happy and healthy without going outside, as long as its home gives it what the outdoors would: chances to play and hunt, places to climb and hide, scratching posts, a clean litter tray, fresh water, and company. Many indoor cats live long, healthy lives. What makes an indoor cat unhappy is not the lack of a garden — it is boredom, too little play, and a home with nothing to climb, scratch or explore.

How much play does an indoor cat need each day?

Aim for two or three short play sessions a day, of about 10 to 15 minutes each, using a wand toy or another toy that moves like prey. Short, frequent sessions fit how cats hunt in the wild — a burst of activity, then rest — and they are easier to keep up than one long session. Finish a session by letting the cat catch the toy, then offer a small meal or treat, which copies the hunt, catch and eat pattern. A kitten or a young cat may want more play, and an older cat may want shorter, gentler sessions — adjust to the cat in front of you.

How do I stop my indoor cat scratching the furniture?

Give the cat something better to scratch, in the right place, and make the furniture less appealing. Put a tall, sturdy scratching post near where the cat sleeps and near the furniture it scratches, with both vertical and horizontal options if you can. Reward the cat when it uses the post. Cover the furniture corner temporarily, trim the claws regularly, and never punish scratching — scratching is normal cat behaviour, and the cat needs a place to do it. If scratching suddenly increases, or the cat scratches one spot raw, ask your vet to check for stress, skin problems or pain.

What are signs an indoor cat is unhappy or stressed?

Common signs include hiding much more than usual, eating much less or much more, over-grooming until the fur thins or the skin is sore, spraying or toileting outside the litter tray, aggression, or sleeping far more than usual. A single quiet day is not a worry, but a change that lasts more than a day or two, or any sign of illness — not eating, vomiting, difficulty passing urine, or trouble breathing — needs a vet visit promptly. If the vet finds no health problem, look at the home: more play, more hiding places, a cleaner litter tray and a calmer routine often help.
