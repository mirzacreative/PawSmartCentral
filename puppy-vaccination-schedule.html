Puppy Vaccination Schedule: First-Year Shots

November 16, 2026 • 7 min read • PawSmartCentral

A new puppy is exciting, and also a little nerve-wracking: that tiny dog has almost no protection against the serious diseases that circulate wherever dogs meet. Vaccines are the single most important thing you can do for your puppy in its first year. They train the immune system to recognise dangerous viruses before the puppy meets them in the real world, and they turn diseases that can kill — parvovirus, distemper, rabies — into illnesses your puppy can fight off. The schedule matters as much as the shots themselves: given too early, a vaccine can be blocked by immunity from the mother; given too late, it leaves a gap when the puppy is unprotected. This guide covers the full puppy vaccination schedule — the core shots at 6–8, 10–12 and 14–16 weeks, boosters, rabies timing, optional vaccines, costs, side effects, and what to do if a shot is missed — so you can keep your puppy safe from the first week home.

Why Puppies Need a Series of Shots, Not Just One

Puppies are born with some protection: antibodies passed from their mother through her milk, called maternal antibodies. These protect a very young puppy, but they fade at different times in different puppies — usually between 6 and 16 weeks — and while they are present they can also block a vaccine from working. That is why vets give a series of shots instead of a single injection. Each round is timed so that at least one of them lands after the maternal antibodies have faded, when the vaccine can take full effect. A puppy that gets only one round may look vaccinated on paper and still be unprotected, because that single dose may have been blocked. The series ends at around 14 to 16 weeks, when nearly all puppies have lost their maternal protection and the final booster gives lasting immunity. The same logic explains the booster a year later: it locks in the protection from the puppy series for adult life.

The Core Puppy Vaccination Schedule

Core vaccines are the ones every puppy needs, whatever its breed, lifestyle or location: distemper, adenovirus (hepatitis), parvovirus, and parainfluenza, usually given together as the DHPP combination, plus rabies where the law requires it. The usual schedule is:

  • 6–8 weeks: first DHPP round (distemper, adenovirus, parvovirus, parainfluenza). Your vet will also do a first health check, a faecal test for worms, and start deworming.
  • 10–12 weeks: second DHPP round. Optional vaccines your vet may recommend — Bordetella (kennel cough) and leptospirosis — often start at this visit too.
  • 14–16 weeks: third DHPP round, completing the core series. Leptospirosis and Bordetella boosters are given if those vaccines were started.
  • 12–16 weeks: first rabies vaccine, where required by law. In many places rabies is legally required, and the timing is set by local rules, so your vet will confirm the exact age.
  • 12 months: booster of DHPP and rabies, one year after the puppy series. After this, most adult dogs move to boosters every one to three years, depending on the vaccine and local rules.

If you adopt a puppy older than 16 weeks with no vaccine history, your vet will usually give two rounds of DHPP spaced three to four weeks apart, plus rabies, rather than the full three-round puppy series. If the breeder or shelter already gave some shots, bring the records to the first vet visit — your vet needs to know exactly what was given and when, so the schedule can be continued rather than restarted. What Each Core Vaccine Protects Against

It helps to know what you are actually protecting your puppy from. Parvovirus is the one vets worry about most in young puppies: it causes severe vomiting and diarrhoea, spreads through infected faeces on the ground, and can kill a puppy in days. It survives in soil and on pavements for months, which is why unvaccinated puppies should not walk in public places. Distemper attacks the respiratory, digestive and nervous systems and is often fatal, even with treatment. Adenovirus causes infectious canine hepatitis, damaging the liver and kidneys. Parainfluenza is one of the viruses behind kennel cough, a hacking cough that spreads fast wherever dogs gather. Rabies is almost always fatal once symptoms appear, and it can spread to people — which is why it is legally required in most places. None of these is a minor illness, and none can be treated reliably once a puppy catches it. Prevention is the only dependable answer.

Optional Vaccines: Bordetella, Leptospirosis and Lyme

Beyond the core shots, your vet may recommend extra vaccines based on where you live and how your puppy will spend its time. Bordetella, the kennel cough vaccine, is worth having if your puppy will go to daycare, boarding, training classes or dog parks — anywhere dogs mix closely. It is often given as a nasal spray or an oral dose rather than an injection. Leptospirosis is a bacterial disease spread through water and soil contaminated by wildlife urine; it can damage the kidneys and liver, and it can spread to people. Vets recommend it for puppies that will swim, hike, or live in rural or wet areas, and in regions where the disease is common. Lyme disease vaccine is suggested in areas with heavy tick populations. These are sometimes called non-core or lifestyle vaccines, but that label does not mean unimportant — it means the decision depends on risk. Ask your vet which ones fit your puppy's life, and do not skip one your vet recommends just because it is not on the core list.

Side Effects: What Is Normal After a Puppy Shot

Most puppies have no reaction at all, or only a mild one. A little soreness at the injection site, mild tiredness, and a slightly reduced appetite for a day are normal — your puppy's immune system is doing its job. A small, soft lump at the injection site is also common and usually disappears within a few days. Serious reactions are rare, but you should know the signs: swelling of the face or muzzle, repeated vomiting, difficulty breathing, or collapse within hours of the shot. Those are an emergency — call your vet or an emergency clinic immediately. It is also normal for a puppy to be a bit quieter on vaccination day. Let your puppy rest, offer water and a small meal, and avoid a big play session or a long walk that evening. If your puppy seems unwell for more than 24 hours after a shot, or develops a fever, call your vet rather than waiting it out.

How Much Do Puppy Vaccinations Cost?

Costs vary by country and clinic, but a single DHPP round typically costs much less than treating the diseases it prevents — a few days of hospital treatment for parvovirus can cost many times more than the entire first-year vaccine series. Many clinics offer a puppy package that bundles the three DHPP rounds, deworming, the first vet checks and the rabies vaccine at a set price, which is usually cheaper than paying per visit. Animal shelters and low-cost clinics often run vaccination days at reduced prices, and some charities offer free or subsidised puppy vaccines. Ask your vet for the full first-year estimate at the first visit, including the one-year booster, so there are no surprises. Keep every vaccine certificate your vet gives you — you will need the rabies certificate for licensing, travel, boarding and training classes.

Can My Puppy Go Outside Before the Series Is Finished?

This is the question almost every new owner asks, and the answer is a careful balance. Your puppy needs socialisation in the first 16 weeks — the window when puppies learn that new people, sounds and places are safe — but it is not fully protected until about a week after the final puppy shots. The practical middle ground: your puppy can go outside in low-risk places, such as your own garden or a friend's clean garden, and can be carried in your arms to see the world from a safe height. Avoid dog parks, pet shops, and pavements where many unknown dogs walk, and do not let your puppy sniff other dogs' faeces or drink from shared bowls. Arrange meetings with friendly, healthy, fully vaccinated dogs you know, and take your puppy to puppy classes that require vaccination records. You can give your puppy plenty of new experiences — car rides, different rooms, visitors, household sounds — without putting it on the ground in a high-risk area. What If My Puppy Misses a Vaccination?

Life gets in the way — a holiday, an illness, a missed appointment — and sometimes a puppy's schedule slips. Call your vet as soon as you notice. A short delay of a few days usually just means the next dose is given late, and the series continues as planned. A longer gap of several weeks may mean the series has to be restarted, because the protection from the earlier doses may have faded without being locked in. Do not try to fix it yourself by giving an extra dose or buying a vaccine online — the timing, storage and dose matter, and a badly stored or wrongly given vaccine may not work at all. Your vet will work out the safest corrected schedule, and will usually want to see the puppy for a health check at the same time. The simplest way to avoid the problem is to book the next appointment before you leave each visit, and to keep the vaccine record on your phone so you always know what is due.

The Bottom Line

A puppy's first year runs on a simple schedule: DHPP at 6–8, 10–12 and 14–16 weeks, rabies at 12–16 weeks where required, lifestyle vaccines like Bordetella and leptospirosis where your vet advises them, and a booster at twelve months. Book the first vet visit within a few days of bringing your puppy home, bring any records the breeder or shelter gave you, and keep the vaccine certificates safe. The shots take minutes, the protection lasts for years, and the diseases they prevent are the ones that fill emergency clinics. Your puppy cannot choose its own protection — that part is up to you.

Frequently asked questions

When should a puppy get its first vaccinations?

A puppy's first core vaccinations are usually given at 6 to 8 weeks of age. The first round covers distemper, adenovirus and parvovirus (often called DHPP), and it is followed by booster rounds every 3 to 4 weeks until the puppy is about 16 weeks old. If you adopt an older puppy, your vet will start the series at the first visit instead.

How many rounds of puppy shots are needed?

Most puppies need three rounds of core vaccines — at roughly 6–8 weeks, 10–12 weeks and 14–16 weeks — plus a rabies vaccine at 12 to 16 weeks where required. The rounds are spaced out because immunity passed from the mother fades at different times in different puppies, and the boosters make sure the vaccine takes hold once that protection is gone.

What happens if my puppy misses a vaccination?

Call your vet as soon as you notice. A short delay of a few days usually just means the next dose is given late, but a long gap may mean the series has to be restarted so the puppy is fully protected. Do not try to catch up by giving extra doses yourself — your vet will work out the safest corrected schedule.

Can my puppy go outside before all its vaccinations are done?

Your puppy can go outside in low-risk places — your own garden, or carried in your arms — but should avoid dog parks, pet shops and pavements where many unknown dogs walk until about a week after the final puppy shots, usually around 16 to 17 weeks. Early socialisation still matters, so introduce your puppy to friendly, healthy, vaccinated dogs and new sights and sounds in safe settings.
