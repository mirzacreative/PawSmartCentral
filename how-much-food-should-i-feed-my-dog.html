How Much Food Should I Feed My Dog? Portion Guide

November 24, 2026 • 10 min read • PawSmartCentral

How much food should I feed my dog? It is the simplest feeding question, and the one with no single answer. A small, older, neutered dog that sleeps most of the day needs far less food than a young, active dog of the same weight that runs for an hour every morning, and a growing puppy needs a different pattern again. The amount on the food packet is a starting point for an average dog, not a prescription for your dog. The right portion is the amount that keeps your individual dog at a healthy weight, with steady energy, a glossy coat, and well-formed stools, over weeks — not the amount that empties the bowl fastest, and not the amount a neighbour's dog eats. This guide explains how to find that amount: how to read the packet guide, how size, age, and activity change the picture, how to measure food accurately, how to judge body condition with your hands and eyes, and when to stop adjusting on your own and ask your vet. It is general guidance only, not veterinary advice, and it does not tell you which food to choose. For choosing a food, see our guides to the best dog food for sensitive stomachs, the best dog foods for allergies, the best dog food for puppies, and the best dog food for senior dogs. This guide is about how much of whichever suitable food you have chosen.

Why There Is No Single Right Amount

Two dogs can weigh the same and need quite different amounts of food. The difference comes from the factors that decide how much energy a dog uses in a day: how active it is, how old it is, its breed and body shape, whether it has been neutered, the temperature it lives in, and its individual metabolism. A working dog, a dog that walks and plays for hours, and a dog that does agility or canicross will use more energy than a dog whose main exercise is a short walk around the block. A puppy is building bone, muscle, and organs, and needs food for growth as well as for daily life. A senior dog is usually less active, may have lost muscle, and may need fewer calories, even though its need for good-quality protein and for joint and health support does not go away. A neutered dog often needs somewhat less food than an unneutered dog of the same size, because neutering can lower energy needs and increase appetite, which is a combination that easily leads to gradual weight gain if portions are not adjusted. None of these factors can be read off a packet, because a packet guide is written for a weight band, not for a life.

That is why the most useful question is not "how many grams?" in the abstract, but "how much keeps this dog in good condition?" Body condition is the feedback that tells you whether the amount is right. If a dog is steadily gaining weight on the packet amount, the packet amount is too much for that dog, however correct it looks on paper. If a dog is steadily losing weight, or is always hungry, restless, and losing condition, the amount is too little, or something else is going on that a vet should check. Owners often adjust portions by eye, adding a handful here and a scoop there, and then wonder why the weight keeps creeping up. The answer is usually that the eye is a poor measuring tool, and that small daily extras — treats, table scraps, a second person's secret titbit, the other dog's leftovers — add up to a large surplus over a week. A measured portion, checked against body condition every few weeks, removes the guesswork. It also gives your vet something concrete to work with: instead of "he eats about a bowlful", you can say "he gets this amount of this food, weighed, twice a day, plus these treats", and the vet can advise from facts rather than impressions.

It also helps to separate two jobs that owners often mix up. Choosing the food is one job: is the food complete and balanced, suitable for the dog's life stage, digestible, and well tolerated? Portioning the food is a different job: how much of that food, split into how many meals, with how much else on top? A very good food fed in too large a portion will still cause weight gain, and a mediocre food fed in exactly the right portion will still be mediocre food. Do the two jobs in order. Settle the food first, with your vet's input if the dog has a health condition, a sensitive stomach, or an allergy, using the food-choice guides linked in the introduction. Then work out the portion, using the method in this guide, and keep it steady long enough to judge it. Changing the food and the portion at the same time makes it impossible to know which change caused a result, good or bad. Steadiness is the theme of the whole guide: one food, one measured amount, checked over weeks, adjusted in small steps.

Start With the Packet Guide — Then Adjust

Every complete dog food carries a feeding guide on the packaging, usually a table that gives a daily amount by the dog's weight, and sometimes by activity level or life stage. That guide is the right place to start, because it is written for that specific food. Foods differ in energy density: a rich, high-fat food delivers more energy per gram than a lighter food, so the same dog needs fewer grams of the rich food and more grams of the lighter one. The packet guide already accounts for that, which is why a gram amount taken from one food must never be applied to another food without checking the new packet. Find your dog's weight on the guide, read across to the daily amount, and treat that figure as your starting daily total — the amount for the whole day, not the amount per meal. If the guide gives a range, start in the middle of the range for an average adult dog, towards the lower end for a less active or neutered dog, and towards the upper end for a very active dog, and then let body condition decide the rest.

Split the daily total into the day's meals. For most adult dogs that means two meals, morning and evening. The arithmetic is simple, and it is worth doing on paper once: if the daily total is, for illustration, 300 grams, two meals means 150 grams per meal. If the daily total is 240 grams and you feed three meals, each meal is 80 grams. The point of the example is the method, not the numbers — the numbers will be whatever your food's guide says for your dog's weight, and they will change when you change food, when the dog's weight changes, or when your vet advises a different amount. Weigh the food rather than scooping it. A kitchen scale is the most accurate tool, and it takes seconds. A cup, a mug, or a scoop varies with how full it is, how the kibble settles, and who is doing the scooping, and a difference of a few grams per meal, repeated twice a day, is a difference of hundreds of grams over a month. If you must use a cup, use the same cup, filled the same way, every time, and check it against a scale at least once so you know what your cup actually holds.

Give the starting amount a fair trial of two to four weeks before you judge it. Weigh the dog at the start, note the date and the amount, and weigh again after two weeks and again after four, at the same time of day, on the same scale. Puppies and growing dogs will gain weight during the trial — that is expected — and the question for a puppy is whether the growth is steady and the puppy looks well, not whether the scale stayed still. For an adult dog, the scale should stay roughly steady, and the body condition checks in the next sections should stay good. If the weight is climbing, reduce the daily amount by a small step — roughly a tenth — and try again for two weeks. If the weight is falling and the dog looks lean, increase by a similar small step. Small, patient adjustments beat large, sudden ones, because a dog's weight responds slowly, and because a large cut can leave a dog genuinely hungry and miserable, which helps nobody. Keep a short note of the amount, the weight, and the date each time you weigh. A notebook, a phone note, or a line on the food bag will do. The note is what turns "I think he is getting bigger" into "he has gained half a kilo in three weeks on this amount", and that is the kind of fact a vet can act on.

Portions by Size: Small, Medium, Large, and Giant Dogs

Size matters for portions in two ways: the total amount, and the pattern of feeding. A small dog eats a small total amount, but that amount is large relative to its body weight, because small dogs have faster metabolisms and lose heat more quickly for their size. A large dog eats a large total amount, but less per kilo of body weight. That is why the packet guide is a table by weight rather than a single figure, and why the amount does not simply double when the dog's weight doubles. It also matters for the stomach: a small dog has a small stomach, and a large meal can be uncomfortable, so small dogs often do better on two smaller meals, or even three, rather than one large one. A large or giant dog, especially a deep-chested breed, should also not be fed one very large meal, and should not exercise hard immediately before or after eating. Two meals a day is the usual pattern for adult dogs of every size, and it is the pattern this guide assumes unless your vet advises otherwise.

For small dogs, the danger is not the size of the portion but the size of the extras. A small dog's daily amount may be only a small bowlful, and a single biscuit, a piece of cheese, or a shared snack can be a large fraction of that day's food. Owners of small dogs often feed generously because the amounts look tiny, and the dog looks as if it has hardly eaten. Resist that feeling, and trust the scale and the body condition instead. Measure the food, count the treats, and remember that a small dog that is a little overweight is carrying a large burden relative to its frame. Our breed profiles give a sense of the expected adult size for many breeds, which helps you judge whether your dog is near its expected weight, although mixed-breed dogs and individual variation mean the breed profile is a guide, not a verdict. For medium dogs, the packet guide is usually a good fit, and the main adjustment is activity: a medium dog that runs daily will need more than a medium dog that walks gently, and the difference can be a full step on the packet table. Watch the waist and the ribs, as described below, rather than the bowl.

For large and giant dogs, the portion questions are bigger, and so are the consequences of getting them wrong. A large dog that is overweight puts extra load on joints that are already under strain, and a giant dog that grows too fast as a puppy can develop joint and bone problems. Large-breed puppies in particular should be fed a food and a portion suited to large-breed growth, and should be kept lean rather than plump, with steady rather than rapid weight gain. Our puppy feeding guide explains the life-stage side of that, and your vet can advise on the growth pattern expected for your breed. For adult large dogs, split the daily amount into two meals, keep the amount steady, and weigh regularly, because a kilo gained on a large dog is easy to miss by eye and easy to dismiss as "just a big dog". The same body-condition checks apply at every size: ribs you can feel with light pressure, a waist visible from above, a tuck-up behind the ribs from the side. Size changes the amount. It does not change the checks.

Portions by Age: Puppies, Adults, and Seniors

Age changes both the amount and the pattern of feeding, and it is the factor owners most often get wrong by applying adult logic to a puppy or a senior. Puppies are growing, and growth needs food: energy for building tissue, and the protein, fat, vitamins, and minerals that growth requires. A puppy's portion, per kilo of body weight, is higher than an adult's, and it is split into more meals, because a puppy's stomach is small and its energy needs are high. Young puppies are often fed three or four times a day, moving to three meals and then to two as they grow towards adulthood. The age at which a puppy becomes an adult depends on size: small breeds mature earlier, often by around a year, while large and giant breeds may keep growing well into their second year. During growth, weigh the puppy regularly, watch that it is growing steadily rather than shooting up, and keep it lean rather than rounded. A plump puppy is not a healthy puppy, however appealing it looks, and excess weight during growth is a burden on developing joints. Ask your vet to check the puppy's growth at vaccination visits, and to confirm the portion and the meal frequency for your puppy's breed and age. Our guide to the best dog food for puppies covers the food side, including why a puppy food, rather than an adult food, is the right choice during growth, and our puppy vaccination schedule explains the vet visits at which growth can be checked.

Adult dogs are the simplest case, and the case the packet guide is written for: a steady daily amount, split into two meals, adjusted for activity and body condition, and reviewed every few weeks. The adult years are when weight gain creeps in, because the dog's activity often falls as it settles, while the portion stays at the level set when it was younger and more active, or when it was first adopted and underweight. A yearly weigh-in at the vet, and a monthly check at home, will catch that creep before it becomes a problem. If the dog's routine changes — a move to a flat without a garden, a new job that means shorter walks, an injury that limits exercise — adjust the portion at the same time, rather than waiting for the weight to follow. The same applies in the other direction: a dog that takes up a new activity, a longer daily walk, or a sport will need more food, and the increase should be gradual, with the same body-condition checks. Adult feeding is a steady routine, reviewed often, not a figure set once and forgotten.

Senior dogs need the same care, in the other direction. An older dog is usually less active, may have lost muscle, and may gain weight more easily on the same amount, so the portion often needs to come down a little, even though the dog's need for good-quality nutrition does not fall. Some senior dogs, on the other hand, lose weight because of dental problems, illness, or a reduced appetite, and need a vet check rather than simply more food. A senior dog that is losing weight, eating less, drinking more, or showing new symptoms should be seen by a vet before the portion is changed, because the cause may be medical. Our guide to the best dog food for senior dogs explains the nutritional side of older-dog feeding, including the life-stage food choice, and the portion should be worked out with the vet alongside it. For seniors, smaller, more frequent meals can help a dog that finds a large meal difficult, or that has a poor appetite, and softening dry food with warm water can help a dog with sore teeth. Keep the routine steady, keep the weighing regular, and treat any unexplained change in weight or appetite in an older dog as a reason to call the vet, not as a reason to adjust the bowl on your own.

Activity, Breed, Neutering, and Health

Activity is the biggest day-to-day variable after weight. A dog that walks for an hour, plays off-lead, swims, or trains regularly uses more energy than a dog that walks for twenty minutes and sleeps the rest of the day, and the difference shows up in the bowl. The packet guide sometimes offers an activity column — a lower amount for a less active dog and a higher amount for an active one — and if yours does, use it as the starting point. If it does not, start in the middle of the weight range and adjust by body condition. Be honest about the activity level. Owners often overestimate how active their dog is, because the walk feels long to the owner, or because the dog is lively in the house. A lively temperament is not the same as sustained exercise, and a dog that is restless indoors may be under-exercised, bored, or asking for food out of habit rather than hunger. Watch the body condition over two to four weeks at a given amount before concluding the amount is wrong, and change only one variable at a time: if exercise increases and the portion increases on the same day, you will not know which change mattered.

Breed and body shape matter too, in a general way. Some breeds are naturally lean and leggy, and look thin to an owner used to a stockier breed. Others are naturally stocky, with a broad chest and a thick coat, and look heavier than they are. A thick coat hides the waist, so a long-coated dog needs a hands-on check rather than a visual one. Breed profiles, such as those in our Breed Encyclopedia, give a sense of the shape and size to expect, but the body-condition checks below are the real test, whatever the breed. Neutering is a common turning point: after neutering, many dogs need somewhat less food and feel hungrier, and owners who keep the same portion often see steady weight gain over the following months. If your dog has been neutered, reduce the portion a little, watch the weight closely for the first few months, and ask your vet for a target amount at the post-neutering check. Health conditions change the picture more fundamentally. A dog with a thyroid problem, diabetes, kidney disease, heart disease, or a digestive condition may need a particular food and a particular portion, and in those cases the vet's advice overrides any general guide, including this one. A dog on medicine should also be checked, because some medicines increase appetite or weight, and the portion may need to be adjusted while the medicine is being taken. If your dog has a sensitive stomach, see our sensitive-stomach guide for the food and transition advice, and work out the portion with your vet alongside it. If an allergy is suspected, see our allergies guide, and do not start an elimination diet or a restricted portion without veterinary guidance.

Measuring Accurately: Scales, Cups, Treats, and Scraps

Accurate measuring is the least glamorous part of feeding, and the part that makes the biggest difference. Weigh the food on kitchen scales, in grams, every time. It takes less than a minute, and it removes the largest source of error in home feeding. If you feed wet food, weigh the tin or pouch contents too, or use the whole tin or pouch as the unit and be consistent about it. If you feed a mix of wet and dry food, weigh both parts, and work out the total against the packet guides for both foods, with your vet's help if the mix is complicated. Keep the scales by the food, so that measuring becomes part of the routine rather than a chore that gets skipped on busy mornings. If more than one person feeds the dog, agree the amount together, write it on a note by the food, and use the same scoop or the same scale, because two people feeding "about the same" can easily feed half as much again between them. A simple chart on the fridge — morning amount, evening amount, treats allowed — prevents double-feeding and prevents the dog from persuading each person in turn that it has not been fed.

Treats are food, and they must be counted as part of the daily total. A useful rule of thumb, widely used by vets as general guidance, is that treats and extras should make up only a small share of the day's food — roughly a tenth — with the main food providing the rest, so that the dog's diet stays complete and balanced. In practice, that means keeping treats small, using a few pieces of the dog's own kibble as training treats where you can, and deducting the treat amount from the main meals if treats have been generous on a particular day. Table scraps are the worst form of extra: fatty, salty, or seasoned human food can upset a sensitive stomach, and some human foods are dangerous for dogs. If scraps are given at all, they should be plain, dog-safe foods, in tiny amounts, and counted. The same applies to chews, dental sticks, and flavoured supplements: they carry calories, and a large chew every day is a significant part of the daily total. Read the label on treats and chews, note the amount, and include it in the day's reckoning. A dog that is fed the right amount of its main food, plus a generous handful of treats, plus a chew, plus a scrap from every plate, is not being fed the right amount. It is being fed the right amount plus a second, unmeasured meal.

Body Condition: The Check That Tells You the Truth

Body condition is how you judge whether the portion is right, and it is a hands-on check, not a number on a scale alone. A scale tells you the weight. Body condition tells you what the weight is made of, and whether the dog looks and feels as it should for its breed and size. Learn the check, and do it every two weeks, at the same time as a weigh-in, so that you build a picture over time. Stand back and look at the dog from above: you should see a waist, a narrowing behind the ribs. Look from the side: you should see a tuck-up, a rise in the belly line behind the ribs, rather than a straight or sagging line. Then put your hands on the dog. Run your hands over the ribs with light pressure, as if you were feeling for a pencil under a thin cloth: you should be able to feel the ribs easily, under a thin layer of fat, without pressing hard. If you have to press firmly to find the ribs, or cannot feel them at all, the dog is probably carrying too much fat. If the ribs, spine, and hip bones are clearly visible, with little covering, the dog is probably too thin. Feel the waist and the belly, and check the muscle over the shoulders and thighs, which should feel firm rather than soft and wasted.

Use the check as a trend, not as a single verdict. A dog that is a little lean after an active holiday, or a little rounder after a quiet winter, is not a crisis. A steady drift in one direction, over a month or more, is information, and it is the signal to adjust the portion by a small step and to check again in two weeks. Take a photograph from above and from the side once a month, in the same light and the same position, if you find it hard to judge by eye. Photographs are surprisingly honest, and they show a slow drift that the daily eye misses. Weigh the dog on the same scale, at the same time of day, and note the weight with the date. Home scales work for small dogs, held in the owner's arms with the owner's weight subtracted. For larger dogs, use the vet's scale at a routine visit, or a pet shop or vet practice that offers a free weigh-in. If the weight and the body condition disagree — the scale says steady, but the dog looks rounder, or the scale says up, but the dog looks leaner — trust the hands-on check and ask your vet to assess the dog, because muscle, coat, and fluid can all affect what the scale says. A vet can score body condition formally, and can tell you the target weight range for your individual dog, which is more useful than any breed-average figure.

Meal Frequency, Routine, and the Hungry Dog

Most adult dogs do well on two meals a day, morning and evening, at roughly the same times each day. Puppies need more frequent, smaller meals, as explained above, and some senior dogs do better on smaller, more frequent meals too. One meal a day is not recommended for most dogs, and it is best avoided for large, deep-chested breeds, because a single large meal is harder to digest and may be linked to bloating, a serious emergency in which the stomach fills with gas and may twist. If your dog is currently fed once a day, splitting the same daily total into two meals is a simple, safe change, and it often improves satiety: a dog that is fed twice is less likely to be ravenous by evening, and less likely to gulp its food. Feed in the same place, in the same bowl, at the same times, and give the dog time to eat in peace, away from other pets if it is rushed or guarded at the bowl. Remove the bowl after twenty to thirty minutes, whether it is empty or not, so that the dog learns the meal has a beginning and an end, and so that you can see how much was actually eaten. Leaving food down all day — free feeding — makes it impossible to know how much the dog ate, and it is the feeding pattern most likely to lead to gradual weight gain.

A dog that always seems hungry is a common worry, and hunger is not always a sign of too little food. Some dogs are hungry by temperament: they would eat all day if allowed, and they will ask, beg, and scavenge regardless of the amount in the bowl. Some dogs are hungry because the food is low in fibre or protein and does not satisfy them, or because they are fed once a day and are genuinely hungry by evening. Some are hungry because of a medical cause, including diabetes, parasites, or a medicine that increases appetite, and a dog that is suddenly much hungrier, or that is hungry and losing weight at the same time, should be seen by a vet. Before increasing the portion for a hungry dog, check the basics: is the portion measured, are treats counted, is the dog getting two meals, is the food complete and suitable, and is the body condition good? If the answers are yes, and the dog is in good condition, the hunger may be habit rather than need, and the answer is routine and distraction — a walk, a game, a chew — rather than more food. If the food seems not to satisfy the dog, ask your vet whether a food with more fibre, or a different formulation, would suit, rather than simply adding more of the same food. And if the dog shares a home with a cat, remember that cat food is richer than dog food, and a dog that steals the cat's food is eating a second, unmeasured meal: feed the cat where the dog cannot reach it, and see our kitten feeding guide and our indoor-cat guides for the cat side of the household.

Adjusting the Portion: Small Steps, Steady Checks

Adjust the portion only on the evidence of the scale and the body-condition check, and only in small steps. If the dog is gaining weight, reduce the daily amount by roughly a tenth, keep everything else the same — same food, same treats, same exercise — and check again in two weeks. If the dog is still gaining, reduce by another small step. If the dog is losing weight and looks lean, increase by roughly a tenth, and check again in two weeks. If the dog is losing weight rapidly, or is losing weight despite eating well, do not keep increasing the food. Book a vet visit, because rapid or unexplained weight loss can be a sign of illness, dental pain, parasites, or a problem absorbing food, and adding more food will not fix the cause. The same applies to a dog that is gaining weight rapidly despite a measured portion: ask your vet to check for a medical cause before cutting the food further. A portion that is cut too far, too fast, can leave a dog hungry, low in energy, and lacking in nutrients, and it can also cause begging and scavenging that undo the cut. Slow and steady is not just kinder. It is more informative, because you can see the effect of each step.

Seasonal and life changes deserve a portion review too. A dog that is less active in winter, or that spends more time indoors during a hot summer, may need a little less food. A dog that starts a new activity, or that moves to a colder climate or an outdoor life, may need a little more. A dog recovering from surgery or illness, a pregnant or nursing dog, and a dog on a new medicine all need veterinary guidance on portions, and the general advice in this guide should not be applied to them without that guidance. Pregnant and nursing dogs, in particular, have sharply increased needs that change week by week, and they should be fed and portioned on veterinary advice. Keep the note of amount, weight, and date through every change, so that you and your vet can see the pattern. The note is the difference between adjusting by guesswork and adjusting by evidence, and it is the habit that makes every later feeding decision easier.

Common Portion Mistakes

  • Feeding the packet amount per meal, not per day. The packet guide is a daily total. Feeding the full daily amount at each meal doubles or triples the intake.
  • Scooping instead of weighing. A cup or mug varies every time. Weigh the food, at least until you know exactly what your scoop holds.
  • Forgetting treats, chews, and scraps. Treats and extras are food. Count them, keep them small, and deduct generous treat days from the main meals.
  • Feeding every person in the house separately. Two people feeding "a little" each can double the portion. Agree one amount, write it down, and stick to it.
  • Judging by the empty bowl. A dog that finishes its bowl is not necessarily still hungry, and a dog that leaves food is not necessarily ill — although a sudden change in appetite should be checked by a vet.
  • Changing food and portion together. Change one thing at a time, so you can tell what caused a change in weight, stools, or condition.
  • Cutting the portion sharply. Large, sudden cuts leave a dog hungry and can miss a medical cause. Adjust in small steps, and check every two weeks.
  • Using another dog's amount. A neighbour's dog, a littermate, or a dog of the same breed may need a different amount. Portion for the dog in front of you.
  • Ignoring the scale. The eye misses a slow drift. Weigh monthly, check body condition fortnightly, and keep a note.
  • Applying adult portions to puppies or seniors. Puppies and seniors need a different pattern, and often a different food. Ask your vet, and see the life-stage guides linked in this post.

When to Ask Your Vet About Portions

Ask your vet about portions at the first visit with a new dog, at puppy vaccination visits, at the post-neutering check, and at every routine check-up, because the vet can weigh the dog, score its body condition, and set a target weight. Ask sooner if any of the following apply: the dog is losing weight without a reason, gaining weight despite a measured portion, suddenly much hungrier or much less interested in food, drinking much more than usual, vomiting or having diarrhoea that keeps returning, or showing signs of pain, lethargy, or illness. Puppies, senior dogs, pregnant or nursing dogs, and dogs with a health condition should have their portions set with veterinary guidance, not from a general guide. A vet can also advise on the right food for a dog with a sensitive stomach, an allergy, or a medical condition, and on how to portion a prescription diet if one is needed. Bring your note — the food, the weighed daily amount, the treats, the weight record, and the dates — to the appointment. It will make the vet's advice specific to your dog, rather than general, and it will save time. General guidance, including this guide, is a starting point for a conversation with your vet, not a substitute for it.

The Bottom Line

How much food should you feed your dog? Start with the feeding guide on your food's packaging for your dog's weight, split that daily total into two meals, weigh the food, count the treats, and then let your dog's body condition and weight over the next two to four weeks tell you whether the starting amount is right. Adjust in small steps, roughly a tenth at a time, and check again. Puppies need smaller, more frequent meals and a food suited to growth. Seniors often need a little less, and a vet check if weight or appetite changes. Active, neutered, and unwell dogs all need the portion fitted to them, not to an average. Keep the routine steady, weigh monthly, check body condition fortnightly, and keep a short note of amount, weight, and date. And when in doubt, when the weight moves fast, or when the dog is very young, very old, or unwell, ask your vet. The right portion is not a number on a packet. It is the amount that keeps your dog, the one in front of you, at a healthy weight, with steady energy, over time. Frequently asked questions

How much food should I feed my dog per day?

There is no single daily amount that suits every dog. Start with the feeding guide printed on your dog's food packaging for your dog's weight, then adjust for age, activity, body condition, and any advice from your vet. Measure the food, split it into the day's meals, count treats as part of the total, and check your dog's weight and body condition every few weeks. If the amount on the packet does not match your dog's condition, adjust gradually and ask your vet for a portion suited to your individual dog.

Should puppies eat more often than adult dogs?

Yes, puppies are usually fed smaller meals more often than adult dogs, because their stomachs are small and they are growing quickly. Young puppies often start on three or four meals a day, moving to two meals a day as they approach adulthood, but the exact timing depends on the puppy's age, breed, and size. Our puppy feeding guide explains life-stage feeding in detail. Ask your vet to confirm the meal frequency and portion for your puppy, especially for large and giant breeds, which grow over a longer period.

How do I know if my dog is the right weight?

Use your hands and your eyes together, and weigh your dog regularly. You should be able to feel the ribs with light pressure under a thin layer of fat, without pressing hard, and see a waist when viewed from above and a tuck-up behind the ribs when viewed from the side. If the ribs are hard to feel, the waist has disappeared, or weight is climbing steadily, the dog is probably getting more food than it needs. If the ribs and hip bones are clearly visible, the dog may need more food, or a vet check. A vet can assess body condition and rule out health causes before you change portions.

Can I follow the feeding guide on the food packet exactly?

Treat the packet guide as a starting point, not a prescription. Packet guides are written for an average dog of a given weight eating that food, and they cannot account for your dog's activity level, metabolism, age, whether it is neutered, the treats it gets, or any health condition. Start with the guide, measure carefully, watch your dog's body condition and energy over two to four weeks, and adjust the amount up or down in small steps. If you are unsure, or if your dog has a health condition, is very young, very old, pregnant, or underweight, ask your vet for an individual recommendation.
