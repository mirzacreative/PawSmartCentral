How to Introduce a New Cat to Your Home Safely

November 26, 2026 • 14 min read • PawSmartCentral

Bringing a new cat home is a beginning, not a single event. The cat arrives in a place that smells unfamiliar, sounds unfamiliar, and may already belong — in the cat's mind — to another cat, a dog, or a busy household. The kindest introduction is therefore a slow one: a safe room first, scent before sight, sight before touch, and short, supervised meetings before free access to the whole home. This guide explains that staged approach in plain language: how to set up the safe room, how scent swapping works, how to plan a realistic timeline, how to stage meetings with a resident cat, a resident dog, and children, which signs of stress mean slowing down, and which problems mean asking a vet for advice. It is general information only, not veterinary or behaviour advice for an individual cat, and it does not replace a vet's judgement. If a cat stops eating, seems unwell, is injured, or shows persistent fear or aggression, contact your vet promptly. For feeding a young newcomer, see our guide to the best cat food for kittens; for the everyday environment once the cat is settled, see our guide to keeping a cat happy indoors; and for an older newcomer, our senior cat care checklist covers the extra care older cats often need. The introduction itself, however, is its own job, and it deserves its own pace.

Before Arrival Day: Plan the Room, Not Just the Welcome

The most useful preparation happens before the cat arrives. Choose one quiet room as the safe room, ideally a room with a door that closes, away from the busiest part of the home, and away from the resident pet's favourite resting places if there is one. The room does not need to be large, but it does need to be complete: a litter tray, food and water bowls placed apart from each other and away from the litter tray, a scratching post or pad, a covered hiding place such as a carrier with the door left open or a cardboard box with an entrance cut in it, a comfortable bed, and a few toys. A window perch or a tall piece of furniture the cat can climb onto gives the cat a choice of height, and cats often feel safer when they can choose to be up high or tucked away. Keep the room calm and predictable: avoid loud music, keep visitors out at first, and keep the door closed so that the new cat and any resident pet cannot meet by accident in the first hours. If the room has a gap under the door, a rolled towel can block sight lines while still allowing scent to pass underneath, which is a gentle first step in scent swapping.

Prepare the rest of the home at the same time, so that the resident animals are not suddenly deprived of everything they know. If a resident cat's litter tray, food bowl, or bed will need to move to make room for the newcomer, move it a few days before arrival, so the resident cat can adjust to the change without associating it with the new cat. Buy the new cat's own equipment rather than sharing the resident cat's bowls and trays from the start: separate litter trays, separate bowls, and separate scratching posts reduce competition and make it easier to tell which cat is eating, drinking, and using the tray normally. Stock the food the new cat is already eating, if you know it, and change food gradually if a change is needed, following the advice in the kitten food guide linked above and your vet's advice, rather than changing food and home on the same day. Finally, agree the household plan in advance: who will look after the new cat, who will supervise meetings, how children will be involved, and what the daily routine will be. An introduction that depends on one tired person improvising each evening is harder than one that the whole household understands from the start.

Arrival day itself should be quiet and short. Bring the cat home in a secure carrier, carry the carrier into the safe room, close the door, and open the carrier there, then leave the cat alone to come out in its own time. Do not tip the cat out, do not pull it out, and do not invite the whole household in to meet it in the first hour. Sit quietly in the room for a while if you wish, speaking softly and letting the cat approach you, but be ready to leave the cat alone if it prefers to stay hidden. Some cats explore within minutes. Others stay in the carrier or behind furniture for hours, and a few stay hidden for a day or more. Hiding at first is a normal way for a cat to cope with a new place, provided the cat is eating, drinking, and using the litter tray, and provided the hiding lessens over days rather than worsening. Leave food, water, and the litter tray where the cat can reach them without crossing open space, check quietly at intervals, and note what the cat has eaten and whether the tray has been used. That simple record — food eaten, water drunk, tray used — is the clearest early evidence that the cat is settling, and it is also the record a vet will ask about if you need advice.

The Safe Room: What It Is For, and How Long It Lasts

A safe room is not a punishment and it is not a permanent arrangement. It is a small, controllable territory where the new cat can learn the smells, sounds, and routines of the home without having to defend a whole house at once, and where the resident animals can learn that a new cat exists without having to share their resting places at once. The safe room gives the new cat time to eat, sleep, and use the tray in peace, and it gives the owner time to observe the cat's normal behaviour — appetite, toileting, play, and sleep — before the complications of meetings begin. It also gives the owner a place to retreat the new cat to at any later stage. If a meeting goes badly, if a resident pet becomes distressed, or if the household becomes too busy for a day, the safe room is still there, and going back to it is not a failure. It is the plan working as intended: the cat returns to a place it already knows is safe, and the introduction pauses until everyone is calm again.

How long the safe room stage lasts depends on the cat, not on the calendar. A confident, social cat that has lived with other cats before may be ready for scent swapping within a day or two and for staged meetings within a week. A shy cat, a cat that has never lived with other animals, a cat that has had a difficult journey, or a cat that is unwell may need two weeks or longer in the safe room before meetings begin, and may need longer still before sharing the whole home. The signs that the safe room stage is working are practical: the cat eats and drinks normally, uses the litter tray reliably, comes out of hiding to explore the room, plays or shows curiosity, and rests in the open rather than only in hiding. The signs that more time is needed are equally practical: the cat hides almost all the time, eats little or nothing, avoids the tray, or shows persistent fear whenever a person enters the room. If a cat is not eating, or if it seems unwell in any way, contact your vet promptly rather than waiting for the introduction to sort itself out, because a cat that is unwell cannot settle, and a health problem should be checked before meetings are attempted.

While the cat is in the safe room, spend time there every day, but on the cat's terms. Sit on the floor, read or talk quietly, offer a toy or a treat, and let the cat decide how close to come. Avoid reaching into a hiding place, avoid picking the cat up before it is comfortable being handled, and avoid staring directly at a frightened cat, which many cats read as a threat. Short, calm visits several times a day are better than one long, intense visit, and a routine — feeding at the same times, visiting at similar times, cleaning the tray quietly — helps the cat to predict the day, which itself reduces stress. Keep the room clean, keep fresh water available, and keep a note of the cat's appetite and toileting, as above. If the cat is a kitten, the kitten food guide linked earlier covers feeding in detail, and if the cat is older, the senior checklist covers the checks that are worth discussing with a vet at the first visit. In both cases, book a vet visit early in the safe room stage, so that health, vaccination, flea and worm treatment, and any behaviour concerns can be discussed before the cat meets the rest of the household.

Scent Swapping: Meeting by Smell Before Meeting by Sight

Cats live in a world of scent in a way people do not. A cat knows its territory, its companions, and its threats largely by smell, and a new cat's scent, arriving suddenly in the middle of the home, can be more alarming to a resident cat than the sight of the cat itself. Scent swapping uses that fact gently: each cat meets the other's smell, in its own space, at its own pace, before the cats meet face to face. The simplest method is to swap bedding. After a day or two, take a blanket or towel the new cat has slept on and place it in the resident cat's area, and place a blanket the resident cat has slept on in the new cat's safe room, where each cat can sniff it, ignore it, or sleep on it as it chooses. A second method uses a soft cloth: rub the cloth gently on one cat's cheeks and head, where scent glands are, and leave the cloth near the other cat's resting place, again where the cat can approach or ignore it freely. Repeat the swap daily, with fresh cloths, so that each cat's scent becomes familiar background rather than a sudden alarm.

Watch the reaction to the swapped scent, because the reaction is the information the next stage depends on. Calm sniffing, a brief interest followed by ignoring the cloth, rubbing against it, or sleeping on the bedding are encouraging signs, and they suggest the cats may be ready for the next stage, which is sight at a distance. Hissing at the cloth, growling, avoiding the area where the cloth lies, spraying near it, or refusing to enter the room where the bedding has been placed are signs that more time is needed at the scent stage, and the next stage should wait. Never force a cat to smell a cloth, never hold a cloth to a cat's face, and never rub a cloth on a cat that is frightened or that pulls away, because forcing the meeting defeats its purpose: the cat must learn that the new scent is safe, and it can only learn that by choosing to approach it. If either cat reacts strongly to scent for many days, or if the reaction worsens rather than easing, ask your vet or a qualified behaviour professional for advice, and do not move on to visual meetings in the hope that seeing the other cat will resolve a scent problem. It rarely does, and it often makes the problem worse.

Scent swapping also works through the home itself, without cloths. When the new cat is settled in the safe room, and the resident cat is in another part of the home, swap the spaces for a short period: let the new cat explore a room the resident cat has used, while the resident cat explores the safe room, with no face-to-face meeting. Keep the swap short at first, supervise by being nearby, and return each cat to its own space before either becomes distressed. This room swap lets each cat meet the other's scent in context — on furniture, on door frames, on scratching posts — which is closer to how cats actually encounter each other in a shared home. It also lets the new cat learn the layout of the home gradually, one room at a time, which makes the later stage of free access less overwhelming. Do the room swap only when both cats are calm about swapped bedding, keep food and litter trays in their usual places during the swap so that neither cat is confused about where to eat or toilet, and stop the swap at once if either cat shows persistent fear or aggression. The aim, at every stage, is a calm meeting, not a fast one.

A Realistic Timeline: Stages, Not Dates

Guides often give a timeline in days, and owners often treat it as a promise. A more honest approach is to think in stages, and to move from one stage to the next only when the signs at the current stage are calm. The stages in this guide are: settling in the safe room; scent swapping; sight at a distance, with a barrier; short supervised meetings in the same room; longer supervised time together; and free access to the home, with retreats still available. Each stage may take days, and the whole process may take two weeks for a confident pair of cats, or six to eight weeks — or longer — for a shy cat, for a cat that has had a bad experience with other cats, or for a household with several resident animals. That range is normal, and it is not a sign that the introduction is failing. The sign that the introduction is failing is not slowness. It is escalation: conflict that worsens, a cat that stops eating, or a cat that is injured. Slowness, with calm behaviour at each stage, is the introduction working.

  • Stage 1 — Settle: safe room only, quiet visits, eating, drinking, and toileting normally, exploring the room, resting in the open at least some of the time.
  • Stage 2 — Scent: swapped bedding and cloths, then short room swaps, with calm sniffing and no persistent hissing, growling, or avoidance.
  • Stage 3 — Sight: seeing each other through a barrier, such as a baby gate or a partly open door, at a distance, with food or play as a calm distraction, and no charging, lunging, or prolonged staring.
  • Stage 4 — Meet: short meetings in the same room, supervised, with escape routes and hiding places, ending while both cats are still calm.
  • Stage 5 — Share: longer supervised time together, meals eaten in the same room at a comfortable distance, play and rest in shared spaces.
  • Stage 6 — Live together: free access to the home, with the safe room still available, separate resources kept in place, and meetings still supervised when the household is busy or visitors arrive.

Two habits make a staged timeline work in practice. The first is to end every meeting while it is still going well, rather than waiting until it goes wrong. A five-minute meeting that ends calmly teaches both cats that meetings end calmly. A twenty-minute meeting that ends in a chase teaches both cats that meetings end in a chase. The second habit is to keep a short daily note: the date, the stage, how long the meeting lasted, what each cat did, and whether food was eaten. The note takes a minute, and it prevents the common error of relying on memory, which tends to remember the worst moment and forget the steady progress. If a stage needs to be repeated, the note shows how many calm repetitions have already happened, which is encouraging, and it shows the vet or behaviour professional, if advice is needed, exactly what has been tried and for how long. A timeline measured in calm repetitions, not in days, is a timeline the cats can actually meet.

Staged Meetings With a Resident Cat

When scent swapping has gone calmly, the next stage is sight at a distance, with a barrier between the cats. A baby gate, a screen, or a door held partly open with a door stop can serve as the barrier, provided neither cat can push through it, jump over it, or get stuck in it. Place the cats on opposite sides of the barrier, at a distance where both can see each other without reacting strongly, and give each cat something pleasant to do: a meal, a treat, or a play session with a person. The food or play is not a bribe. It is a way of pairing the sight of the other cat with a calm, ordinary activity, so that the other cat's presence becomes part of the background rather than an alarm. Start with the cats far apart, keep the first sighting very short — a minute or two — and end it while both cats are still calm, even if that feels too short. Repeat daily, gradually reducing the distance only when both cats remain calm at the current distance, and never reduce the distance and increase the duration on the same day.

Watch the body language throughout, and learn the difference between caution and conflict. Caution looks like a slow approach, a pause, ears forward or neutral, a sniff, and a choice to walk away. Conflict looks like a fixed stare, flattened ears, a puffed-up tail and coat, a low growl, a hiss that is repeated, or a lunge. A single hiss, followed by a cat walking away, is often a boundary being set, and it does not necessarily mean the introduction has failed, provided the cats are separated calmly and the next meeting is set at a greater distance. Repeated hissing, chasing, swatting with claws out, or a cat that will not stop staring or advancing means the stage was too much, too soon, and the introduction should go back to scent swapping for a few days. Separate the cats calmly if a meeting escalates: use a loud noise, such as clapping, or a towel placed between them, rather than picking up a cat in the middle of a fight, which risks injury to the person as well as to the cats. After a bad meeting, give both cats a day or more of calm separation before trying again, at an earlier stage, and ask your vet or a qualified behaviour professional for advice if bad meetings keep happening.

When sightings through a barrier are calm, move to short meetings in the same room, with the barrier removed but with escape routes open. Choose a neutral room, not the resident cat's favourite sleeping place, provide a hiding place and a high perch for each cat, and keep the meeting short, supervised, and pleasant, with food, treats, or play. Keep the door open so that either cat can leave, and let either cat leave without following it. Do not hold the cats, do not place them near each other, and do not force them to share a bowl or a bed. Gradually lengthen the meetings, and gradually let the cats eat in the same room, starting at a distance where both eat calmly and moving the bowls closer only when both remain calm. Keep the cats' resources separate even when they are eating in the same room: separate bowls, separate litter trays, and separate resting places, in different parts of the home, for the long term. Cats that share a home peacefully often still prefer separate resources, and competition over a single litter tray or a single food bowl is a common and avoidable cause of tension, as our indoor happiness guide explains in more detail.

Staged Meetings With a Resident Dog

Introducing a new cat to a resident dog follows the same staged logic — scent, then sight at a distance, then short supervised meetings — but the risks and the controls are different, because a dog is larger, faster, and more likely to chase. The safe room stage is therefore even more important: the cat must have a room the dog cannot enter, with a door that closes firmly, and the dog must not be allowed to scratch at, bark at, or stare through the door. Scent swapping works the same way, with bedding and cloths, and the dog's reaction to the cat's scent should be calm curiosity, not fixation, barking, or lunging at the door. If the dog becomes highly excited, barks persistently, or cannot be distracted from the safe room door, more time at the scent stage is needed, and the dog's training — a reliable recall, a reliable settle, and a reliable leave-it — should be practised away from the cat before meetings begin. Ask your vet for advice if the dog shows persistent intense interest in the cat, or if the dog has a history of chasing or harming small animals, because some dog-and-cat combinations need professional guidance, and some are not safe to leave unsupervised at any stage.

The first sighting should be through a sturdy barrier, with the dog on a lead, held by an adult, at a distance where the dog can see the cat without pulling, barking, or fixating, and the cat can see the dog without fleeing in panic. Keep the sighting short, reward calm behaviour in the dog with praise or a treat, and let the cat choose whether to approach the barrier or to retreat to a hiding place. Repeat at the same distance until both animals are calm, then reduce the distance gradually, over days, not in one session. The first meeting in the same room should be equally controlled: the dog on a lead, an adult holding the lead, a second adult near the cat, escape routes open, and a high perch or a hiding place the cat can reach without crossing the dog's path. Keep the meeting short, end it while both animals are calm, and never leave the cat and the dog unsupervised together until many calm meetings have happened and you are confident that the dog will not chase and the cat will not panic. Even then, separate the animals when the household is out, when visitors arrive, or when the dog is over-excited, and keep the cat's safe room available as a permanent retreat. A dog that is calm with a cat when supervised may still chase when unsupervised, and a cat that is calm with a dog in one room may still panic in a hallway. Supervision, at every stage, is the rule that keeps both animals safe.

Introducing a New Cat to Children

Children and a new cat can become good companions, but the introduction needs the same staged patience as an introduction to another animal, with the added need to teach the child what the cat needs. Do not arrange a first meeting with children on arrival day. Let the cat settle in the safe room first, then introduce children calmly, one or two at a time, with an adult present, sitting quietly on the floor and letting the cat choose whether to approach. Teach children, in advance, the rules that keep the meeting safe for both sides: move slowly, speak quietly, do not run after the cat, do not pick the cat up, do not pull its tail or ears, do not corner it, and leave it alone when it walks away, hides, or shows the signs of stress described in the next section. A child who understands that the cat chooses the meeting will have better meetings than a child who is told only to be gentle, because the child will learn to read the cat's choice. Praise the child for waiting, for sitting still, and for letting the cat come to them, and make the first meetings short and pleasant, with a toy the child can use to play with the cat at a distance, such as a wand toy, rather than hands-on play.

Keep the safe room a child-free retreat at first, so that the cat always has a place where it will not be followed, picked up, or surprised. As the cat settles, let children join the calm visits to the safe room, under adult supervision, and let them help with the routine: filling the water bowl, offering a treat, or playing with a wand toy, always under supervision. Involving children in the routine, rather than only in the exciting first meeting, teaches responsibility and gives the cat a predictable experience of children as calm, routine people rather than as sudden excitement. Supervise every early meeting, without exception, and keep supervising until the cat is clearly comfortable with each child individually, and each child is reliably calm with the cat. If the cat shows persistent fear of children, hides whenever a child enters the room, or swats or bites when approached, ask your vet for advice, and slow the introduction down rather than pushing the cat to accept more contact. A cat that is given time to choose contact will usually choose it. A cat that is forced into contact will usually avoid the child, and may defend itself, which helps nobody.

Signs of Stress, and When to Slow Down

Stress in cats is often quiet, and the signs are easy to miss if the household is looking only for fighting. The signs below are not diagnoses, and they do not, on their own, prove that a cat is ill or that an introduction has failed. They are signals to slow down, to go back a stage, and to seek veterinary advice if they continue or worsen.

  • Hiding for long periods, beyond the first day or two, or hiding that increases rather than lessens as the days pass.
  • Refusing food or water, eating much less than usual, or drinking much more or much less than usual.
  • Toileting outside the tray, spraying, or avoiding a tray that was previously used reliably.
  • Over-grooming, licking or chewing at fur until it thins, or grooming that seems compulsive rather than relaxed.
  • Withdrawal, a cat that stops playing, stops seeking company it previously enjoyed, or stays in one place for most of the day.
  • Aggression or persistent conflict, repeated hissing, growling, swatting, chasing, or blocking another cat's access to food, water, or the tray.
  • Changes in sleep, appetite, or vocalisation, including crying at night, restlessness, or a sudden change in the amount the cat eats or sleeps.

When any of those signs appear, the first response is to slow down, not to push on. Go back one stage — from meetings to sight through a barrier, or from sight to scent swapping — and stay at that stage until the signs ease. Check the practical basics at the same time: is the cat eating, drinking, and toileting normally? Is there a quiet retreat available? Are the resources — food, water, trays, resting places — separate and plentiful? Is the household routine predictable, or has it become noisy and disrupted? If the signs are mild and they ease when the pace slows, the introduction can be resumed cautiously, at the earlier stage, with shorter meetings and greater distances. If the signs continue for more than a few days, if they worsen, if the cat stops eating, if the cat seems unwell, or if any cat is injured, contact your vet promptly. A vet can check for a health problem that is making the cat less able to cope, can advise on pain, illness, or anxiety that needs treatment, and can refer to a qualified behaviour professional if the problem is behavioural. Do not try to treat a stress or behaviour problem at home with a product, a supplement, or a remedy found online without veterinary advice, because the cause needs to be identified first, and a product that masks a sign without addressing its cause can delay the help the cat needs.

Feeding, Litter Trays, and Resources During the Introduction

Resources are the quiet infrastructure of a peaceful multi-cat home, and the introduction is the time to get them right. The general rule many vets and behaviour professionals suggest is one litter tray per cat, plus one extra, placed in different, quiet locations, so that no cat has to pass another cat to reach a tray, and no cat can block another's access. Keep the new cat's tray in the safe room at first, and add trays in other parts of the home as the cat's access expands, rather than moving the safe room tray and confusing the cat. Scoop trays daily, keep them clean, and note any change in toileting, because a change in toileting is often the first sign of stress or illness, as the previous section explains. Feed the cats separately at first, in their own spaces, so that neither cat feels it must compete for food, and move to eating in the same room only gradually, at a distance where both eat calmly. Keep water bowls in several places, away from food bowls and away from litter trays, and keep the new cat's food the same as the food it was eating before arrival, changing it gradually if a change is needed, as the kitten food guide explains.

Scratching posts, resting places, and play opportunities should also be duplicated, not shared by force. Provide a scratching post or pad in the safe room and another in the main living area, so that each cat can scratch where it rests and plays, and provide a high perch and a covered hiding place in each part of the home the cat will use. Play with each cat separately at first, so that each cat gets attention without having to compete for it, and play with the cats together only when meetings are calm and both cats choose to join in. Give the resident cat the same attention it had before the newcomer arrived, and give the newcomer its own attention in the safe room, so that neither cat experiences the introduction as a loss. Jealousy is a human word, and cats do not feel it in the human sense, but cats do notice a change in routine, a loss of attention, or a loss of access to a favourite place, and those losses can show as stress. Keeping the resident cat's routine as stable as possible, while building a new routine for the newcomer, is one of the simplest ways to keep an introduction calm.

Common Mistakes, and How to Avoid Them

  • Meeting on day one. Placing the new cat in the living room with the resident pets on arrival day, in the hope that they will sort it out, is the most common mistake, and it often creates a conflict that takes weeks to undo.
  • Forcing contact. Holding cats near each other, placing a cat in another cat's bed, or forcing a cat to smell a cloth defeats the staged approach, because the cat learns fear, not safety.
  • Moving too fast after one good meeting. One calm meeting is encouraging, not conclusive. Repeat the stage several times before moving on, and end meetings while they are still calm.
  • Sharing resources too soon. One litter tray, one food bowl, or one resting place shared by two cats that have just met invites competition. Keep resources separate and plentiful.
  • Leaving cats or a cat and a dog unsupervised too soon. Supervision should continue until many calm meetings have happened, and separation when the household is out should continue longer still.
  • Punishing a cat for hissing or hiding. Hissing and hiding are communications, not misbehaviour. Punishment increases fear and makes the next meeting worse.
  • Ignoring stress signs. A cat that stops eating, hides constantly, or toilets outside the tray is signalling a problem. Slowing down and seeking veterinary advice is the response, not waiting and hoping.
  • Changing food, home, and routine all at once. Keep the food the cat knows, keep the routine predictable, and change one thing at a time, so that the cat can adjust.
  • Letting children meet the cat unsupervised. Every early meeting with a child needs an adult present, and the safe room needs to stay a retreat the child does not enter uninvited.
  • Giving up after a bad meeting. A bad meeting means going back a stage, not giving up. With patience, and with veterinary or behaviour advice where needed, many introductions that start badly end well.

The Bottom Line

To introduce a new cat to your home safely, start with a complete safe room, let the cat settle and eat, drink, and toilet normally, swap scent before sight, let the cats see each other through a barrier at a distance, and build up to short, supervised meetings that end while everyone is still calm. Introduce a resident dog with the same stages, with a lead, a barrier, and supervision, and introduce children calmly, one or two at a time, with an adult present and the cat free to walk away. Keep resources separate and plentiful, keep the resident cat's routine stable, keep a short daily note, and slow down — going back a stage — whenever stress signs appear. Ask your vet promptly if a cat stops eating, seems unwell, is injured, or shows persistent fear or aggression, and ask for a referral to a qualified behaviour professional if the introduction stalls. Patience is not wasted time. It is the introduction itself, and a slow introduction that ends in a calm, shared home is worth far more than a fast introduction that ends in conflict. Frequently asked questions

How long should a new cat stay in a safe room?

There is no fixed number of days that suits every cat. Many cats settle in a safe room for at least a few days, and some need two weeks or longer before staged meetings begin. Move on only when the new cat is eating, drinking, using the litter tray, and behaving in a relaxed way in the safe room, and when scent swapping has gone calmly. If either cat is still hiding, hissing, or refusing food, stay at the current stage and ask your vet or a qualified behaviour professional for advice if the difficulty continues.

How does scent swapping work when introducing cats?

Scent swapping means letting each cat meet the other's smell before they meet face to face. Swap bedding, or rub a soft cloth gently on one cat's cheeks and leave it near the other cat's resting place, where the cat can approach or ignore it freely. Do this for several days, and watch the reaction: calm sniffing and ignoring the cloth suggest the cats are ready for the next stage, while hissing, growling, or avoiding the cloth suggests more time is needed. Never force a cat to smell a cloth, and never rub a cloth on a cat that is frightened.

What are signs that a cat introduction is going too fast?

Signs that the pace is too fast include persistent hissing, growling, swatting, or chasing, hiding for long periods, refusing food or water, avoiding or spraying outside the litter tray, over-grooming, and a resident cat that stops eating or withdraws from the family. A brief hiss or a flat-eared stare at a first sighting can be part of a cautious meeting, but repeated or escalating conflict, or any sign of illness or injury, means going back a stage. If signs continue, or if a cat stops eating, seems unwell, or is injured, contact your vet promptly.

Can a new cat meet children on the first day?

It is better not to arrange a first meeting with children on arrival day. Let the new cat settle in the safe room first, then introduce children calmly, one or two at a time, with an adult present, sitting quietly and letting the cat choose whether to approach. Teach children to move slowly, speak quietly, avoid picking the cat up, and leave the cat alone when it walks away or hides. Supervise every early meeting, keep the safe room a child-free retreat at first, and ask your vet for advice if the cat shows persistent fear of children.
