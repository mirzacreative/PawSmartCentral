Best Dog Food for Senior Dogs: What to Feed (2026)

November 19, 2026 • 9 min read • PawSmartCentral

Your dog has spent years eating the same adult food, and now things are changing. The walks are shorter. The naps are longer. The waistline is creeping out even though the bowl looks the same. Ageing changes a dog's body in ways that show up at mealtimes: metabolism slows, muscle is harder to keep, joints stiffen, kidneys work less efficiently, and teeth and digestion are not what they were. The food that kept your dog healthy at five is not automatically the right food at ten. The best dog food for senior dogs is not a magic formula or a particular brand — it is a complete and balanced food matched to an older dog's slower metabolism, changing body condition and health, chosen with your vet's input. This guide explains when a dog counts as a senior, what ageing changes about nutrition, what to look for on the label, how to handle weight, joints and kidneys, and how to switch foods safely.

When Is a Dog a Senior?

There is no single birthday when every dog becomes a senior, because dogs age at very different speeds depending on their size. As a rough guide, small breeds such as Dachshunds, Shih Tzus and Toy Poodles are usually considered seniors at around 9 to 10 years old. Medium breeds such as Beagles, Border Collies and Cocker Spaniels are seniors at around 8 to 9. Large breeds such as Labradors and German Shepherds reach senior status at around 7 to 8, and giant breeds such as Great Danes can be seniors as early as 5 to 6. The reason is simple: bigger dogs live shorter lives and age faster, so their senior years start earlier.

But the calendar is only half the story. The real signs of a senior dog are the ones you see at home: a dog that sleeps more and plays less, that is slower to get up after a nap, that gains weight on the same amount of food, that has a greying muzzle or cloudier eyes, or that seems less interested in food or slower to finish a meal. If you are seeing those signs, it is time to talk to your vet about whether the diet should change — and to check your breed's expected senior age in our Breed Encyclopedia. Age alone should never be the only reason to switch foods: a fit, lean eight-year-old with no health problems may do perfectly well on the adult food it already eats, while a six-year-old giant breed with stiff joints may need a senior formula already. The switch is a decision, not a date.

How Ageing Changes a Senior Dog's Nutrition

Ageing changes a dog from the inside out, and almost every change touches the food bowl. The most important shift is metabolism: an older dog burns fewer calories, because it moves less and its body runs a little slower. A dog that ate the same adult food at the same portions will often gain weight — and extra weight on an older dog is not harmless padding, it is extra load on joints that are already wearing out. At the same time, an older dog finds it harder to keep muscle. Muscle is lost more easily with age, and once it is gone it is harder to rebuild, so the protein in the diet matters more, not less.

Joints are the next change. Cartilage thins, joints stiffen, and a dog that used to leap onto the sofa now needs a step or a helping hand. Nutrition cannot repair a worn joint, but the right nutrients can support the tissue around it. Kidneys also work less efficiently with age, which is why phosphorus and protein quality — not just protein quantity — become part of the conversation for older dogs. Digestion slows too: an older gut absorbs nutrients less efficiently and is more easily upset by sudden changes, rich food or too much fat. Teeth wear down, gums recede, and a dog with sore teeth may avoid hard kibble it used to eat happily. Finally, an older dog's sense of smell and taste fades, so food that once smelled irresistible may now seem dull — which is one reason some seniors eat less, not because they are ill but because the food no longer appeals to them. A good senior diet is designed around all of these changes at once: fewer calories, good protein, joint support, gentle digestion and a smell and texture an older dog still wants to eat.

What to Look For on the Label

The label is where a senior food earns its name — or fails to. Start with the life-stage statement: look for wording that says the food is complete and balanced for adult maintenance or specifically for senior dogs, following recognised nutritional guidelines. Be aware that "senior" is not a tightly defined legal term in pet food in the way "puppy" and "adult" are, so a bag that says "senior" may differ a lot from another bag that says the same word. That is why the rest of the label matters more than the word on the front.

Next, read the ingredient list. Look for a named animal protein — chicken, lamb, salmon, beef — in the first one or two positions. Older dogs need protein that is easy to digest and use, and a named protein tells you what animal the food is made from. After the protein, look for a digestible carbohydrate such as rice or oats, a named fat source, and recognisable fruits and vegetables. Then check the guaranteed analysis: protein and fat minimums, fibre and moisture maximums. A senior food is usually a little lower in calories and fat than the adult version of the same food, and a little higher in fibre — that combination helps a less active dog stay full without gaining weight.

Three extras are worth looking for. Omega-3 fatty acids, usually from fish oil, support joints, skin and brain health, and are one of the most useful additions in a senior formula. Glucosamine and chondroitin are often added for joint support — the amounts in food are modest, and they are a support, not a treatment, but they are a reasonable thing to look for. And antioxidants such as vitamin E and vitamin C are often included to support an ageing immune system. What you should not do is choose by the picture on the bag or by words like "premium" or "natural" — those are marketing, not nutrition. Read the life-stage statement, the ingredient list and the guaranteed analysis, in that order. The same label-reading habits apply if your senior dog has a sensitive stomach — our guide to choosing food for dogs with allergies explains how to use an elimination approach and read labels for hidden ingredients.

Senior Food vs Adult Food vs All Life Stages

Many owners wonder whether they need a senior food at all, or whether the adult food their dog already eats is fine. The honest answer is: it depends on the dog. An adult maintenance food is designed for a healthy adult dog at a normal activity level. If your senior dog is lean, active, has no health problems and is holding its weight, there is often no urgent reason to change — a good adult food, fed in the right amount, may be exactly right. A senior formula is essentially an adult food adjusted for an older dog: usually a little lower in calories, a little higher in fibre, with added omega-3s and joint support, and sometimes a softer kibble. It is a good fit when your dog is slowing down, gaining weight, or showing the first signs of stiffness.

An all life stages food is formulated to meet the needs of both puppies and adults, which means it is richer than a senior dog needs — it is built for growth, not for a slowing metabolism. For a senior dog that is gaining weight, an all life stages food is usually the wrong choice. The practical rule is to let the dog, not the bag, decide: if your senior dog is gaining weight, moving less, or showing stiffness, a senior formula is worth trying; if your senior dog is lean, active and healthy, the adult food it already eats may be fine, and your vet can confirm. Never switch foods just because the bag says "senior" — switch because your dog's body has changed.

Joints: Feeding a Dog That Moves More Slowly

Stiff joints are the most visible part of ageing for many dogs, and food is one part of managing them — not a cure, but a support. The single most important thing food can do for joints is keep the dog lean. Every extra kilogram is extra load on hips, knees and elbows that are already wearing out, and weight control matters more than any supplement. A lean senior dog moves better, hurts less and lives longer than an overweight one, and the bowl is where that is decided.

Beyond weight, the nutrients that get talked about most are omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA from fish oil), which are included in many senior foods and are associated with supporting joint comfort, and glucosamine and chondroitin, which are added to many senior formulas. Be honest about what these do: they are a support for the tissue around the joint, not a repair for a worn joint, and the amounts in a bag of food are modest. If your vet thinks your dog needs more, they may suggest a separate supplement at a proper dose — do not try to reach a supplement dose by overfeeding the food. Also, do not add calcium or other minerals to a complete food: a complete senior formula already has the right balance, and extra minerals can do more harm than good, especially if your dog has kidney concerns. If your dog is struggling to get up, limping after rest, or reluctant to climb stairs, that is a vet conversation first and a food conversation second.

Weight Control: The Quiet Senior Problem

Weight gain is the most common nutrition problem in senior dogs, and also the most fixable. An older dog may need around 20 to 30 percent fewer calories than it did as an adult, simply because it moves less. If the portions stay the same, the weight creeps up — slowly, a few hundred grams a month, until the dog is carrying a fifth of its body weight extra. That weight makes joints worse, makes breathing harder, and makes every other health problem harder to manage.

Start with the feeding guide on the bag, but treat it as a starting point: it is written for an average dog of that weight, and your dog's age, breed and activity will shift the right amount. Measure every meal with a cup or a kitchen scale — never guess, and never free-feed an older dog. Use body condition as your real guide: you should be able to feel the ribs easily under a thin layer of fat, see a waist when you look down from above, and see a tucked-up belly from the side. Weigh your dog every couple of weeks and write the number down. Treats count too: keep them small, keep them to a small share of the daily calories, and subtract them from the meals rather than adding them on top. A few training treats a day are fine; a handful of biscuits after every walk is not. If your dog is already overweight, ask your vet for a target weight and a safe rate of loss — crash diets are dangerous for older dogs, and a slow, steady loss is the goal.

When a Senior Dog Loses Weight Instead

Some senior dogs go the other way: they eat less, lose weight, and their ribs and hips start to show. Weight loss in an older dog is not a normal part of ageing and it should never be ignored. It can be a sign of dental pain that makes chewing hard, a kidney or thyroid problem, or another illness that needs treatment. If your senior dog is losing weight, or has stopped finishing meals, see your vet before changing foods. Once the vet has checked your dog, warming the food slightly, adding a spoonful of warm water, or switching to a softer texture can help a fussy senior eat — smell is a big part of appetite for dogs, and warm food smells stronger. Do not tempt a dog that will not eat with rich table scraps or fatty leftovers: they can trigger pancreatitis, and they teach the dog to hold out for something better.

Kidneys and Other Health Conditions

Many senior dogs develop a health condition that changes what they should eat, and this is where general guidance has to stop and your vet has to take over. Kidney disease is the most common one: as kidneys work less efficiently, the amount and quality of protein, and the amount of phosphorus in the diet, become important. A vet may recommend a prescription or veterinary diet with controlled phosphorus and carefully chosen protein — an ordinary senior food from the shelf is not the same thing, and switching to one on your own is not a substitute for veterinary advice. Heart disease can mean watching sodium. Diabetes can mean a food with a steady, controlled carbohydrate supply and strict meal timing. Liver disease, pancreatitis and food sensitivities each have their own dietary rules.

The point is not to memorise every condition — it is to know that a senior dog with a diagnosis needs a diet chosen for that diagnosis, and that the choice should be made with your vet, not in the pet food aisle. If your vet has recommended a specific food, feed that food, and ask them before adding treats, supplements or table scraps, because even a small extra can undo the point of a prescription diet. If your dog has no diagnosis, the general guidance in this article applies — but a senior dog should have a vet check at least once a year, and twice a year once it is well into its senior years, so that a problem is found while it is still small.

Transitioning to a New Food

An older dog's digestion is less forgiving than a young dog's, so a new food should never be introduced overnight. Switch gradually over 7 to 10 days: start with about a quarter of the new food mixed into three quarters of the old food, move to half and half, then three quarters new, then fully new — watching appetite, stools and energy at each step. A few soft stools during a switch are common and usually settle. Persistent diarrhoea, vomiting, or a dog that stops eating is a reason to slow down or go back a step, and if it does not settle, to call your vet.

Switch at a calm time, not during a house move, a holiday, or a course of medication, because stress alone can upset an older dog's stomach and you will not know whether the food or the stress caused it. Keep everything else the same during the switch — the same bowl, the same times, the same treats — so the food is the only thing that changes. If your dog refuses the new food entirely, try warming it slightly or adding a little warm water before deciding the food is wrong: texture and smell matter more to an older dog than they do to a young one. And if you have just brought home a puppy as well as caring for a senior, remember that puppy food and senior food are very different things — our guide to the best dog food for puppies explains what a growing dog needs, and it is not what your senior dog needs.

Wet Food, Dry Food, and Homemade Diets

Both wet and dry food can work well for a senior dog, and many owners feed a mix. Dry food (kibble) is convenient, easy to measure, and the crunch can help keep teeth cleaner — but it is hard, and a senior dog with sore teeth or missing teeth may struggle with it. Wet food is softer, easier to chew, and adds a lot of water to the diet, which supports hydration in an older dog that may not drink enough — but it is more expensive per calorie and spoils once opened. A common approach is dry food for the main meals with a spoonful of wet food mixed in for smell, moisture and variety, or a move to mainly wet food if the dog's teeth make kibble hard work. Soaking kibble in warm water for a few minutes is another simple way to soften it, and it releases more smell, which helps a fussy senior eat.

Homemade and raw diets need a warning of their own. A homemade diet for a senior dog is much harder to get right than it looks: an older dog needs the right balance of protein, phosphorus, calcium and calories, and a home-cooked recipe that is not formulated with veterinary guidance can easily be short of something important or heavy in something harmful. Raw diets carry their own risks, including bacteria that an older dog's immune system is less able to handle. If you want to cook for your senior dog, do it with a recipe from a veterinary nutritionist, not from a blog — and tell your vet what you are feeding, so they can check it properly.

Foods to Avoid

The dangerous foods for dogs do not become safe with age — and a senior dog is more vulnerable to them because of its smaller reserves. Keep these well out of reach:

  • Chocolate — contains theobromine, which dogs cannot process properly. Dark chocolate and baking chocolate are the most dangerous.
  • Grapes and raisins — even a small number can cause kidney failure in dogs, and there is no safe amount.
  • Xylitol (birch sugar) — a sweetener in some sugar-free gum, sweets and peanut butter. It can cause a dangerous drop in blood sugar and liver damage.
  • Onions and garlic — in any form, they can damage red blood cells and cause anaemia.
  • Macadamia nuts — can cause weakness, vomiting and tremors, even in small amounts.
  • Cooked bones — they splinter easily and can injure the mouth, throat or intestines.
  • Alcohol and caffeine — even small amounts can be serious for an older dog.
  • Fatty leftovers — rich scraps can trigger pancreatitis, which is more dangerous in an older dog.

If your senior dog eats any of these, call your vet or an animal poison helpline right away rather than waiting for symptoms — early advice makes a real difference. Mistakes to Avoid

  • Keeping the same portions as the adult years — a senior dog usually needs fewer calories, and the same portions mean steady weight gain.
  • Switching foods overnight — an older gut needs a gradual 7 to 10 day change.
  • Choosing food by the word "senior" on the bag — the term is loosely defined; read the life-stage statement, ingredients and guaranteed analysis instead.
  • Cutting protein too far — older dogs need good-quality protein to keep muscle; a very low-protein food is not the answer unless your vet has specifically advised it for a kidney problem.
  • Adding supplements on top of a complete food — extra calcium or minerals can do more harm than good; ask your vet before adding anything.
  • Feeding puppy food to a senior dog — puppy food is rich, high in calories and minerals, and the opposite of what an older dog needs.
  • Ignoring weight loss — a senior dog that is losing weight needs a vet visit, not a different bag of food.
  • Skipping vet checks — weight, appetite, thirst and stool changes are medical signs first; check them with a vet before changing the diet.

The Bottom Line

The best dog food for senior dogs is a complete and balanced formula with a named animal protein first, a moderate calorie level matched to a less active life, enough high-quality protein to maintain muscle, added fibre for digestion, and omega-3s for joints and brain health — chosen because your dog's body has changed, not just because the bag says "senior". Feed it in measured meals, watch your dog's body condition and weight, switch foods gradually over 7 to 10 days, and keep the dog lean: weight control does more for an older dog's joints and health than any single ingredient. If your dog has a health condition such as kidney disease, the diet should be chosen with your vet, not from the shelf. If your senior dog is gaining or losing weight, refusing food, drinking much more than usual, or showing any sign of illness, ask your vet before changing their diet or routine.

Frequently asked questions

When should I switch my dog to senior dog food?

Most dogs are considered seniors at around 7 to 8 years old, though large and giant breeds age faster and may be seniors at 5 to 6, while small breeds may not reach senior status until 9 or 10. Age alone is not the trigger: switch when your vet agrees your dog is slowing down, gaining weight on the same food, or showing age-related changes, and switch gradually over 7 to 10 days.

What should senior dog food contain?

A good senior dog food is a complete and balanced formula with a named animal protein first, a moderate calorie level matched to a less active dog, enough high-quality protein to maintain muscle, added fibre for digestion, and omega-3 fatty acids for joints and brain health. It should carry a life-stage statement for adult maintenance or for seniors, and the guaranteed analysis should be easy to read on the label.

How much should I feed a senior dog?

Most senior dogs need fewer calories than they did as adults, because they move less and their metabolism slows — often around 20 to 30 percent less. Start with the feeding guide on the bag, measure every meal, and adjust by body condition: you should be able to feel the ribs easily under a thin layer of fat. Weigh your dog regularly and ask your vet to confirm the right daily amount.

What should I do if my senior dog will not eat?

A senior dog that stops eating, eats much less, or loses weight needs a vet visit before you change foods, because appetite loss in an older dog can be a sign of dental pain, kidney trouble or another health problem. Do not wait it out, do not switch foods repeatedly hoping one will work, and do not offer rich table scraps to tempt them. Once the vet has checked your dog, warming the food slightly or adding a little water can help a fussy senior eat.
