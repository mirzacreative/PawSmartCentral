Best Cat Food for Kittens: What to Feed (2026)

November 20, 2026 • 9 min read • PawSmartCentral

A kitten is not a small adult cat. In its first year, a kitten will roughly double and redouble its birth weight many times over, grow its bones, build its brain, and develop an immune system that has to last a lifetime — and every gram of that growth is built from the food in its bowl. The best cat food for kittens is not a smaller portion of adult food, and it is not the prettiest bag on the shelf. It is a complete and balanced food made for growth: higher in calories, protein and fat than adult food, with the right minerals for growing bones and the right nutrients for a developing brain, fed in small meals several times a day. This guide explains how kitten nutrition differs from adult nutrition, what to look for on the label, how much to feed at each age, how weaning works, what to do about fussy eaters and sensitive stomachs, and when to switch to adult food.

Why Kittens Need Different Food

A kitten's body is doing something an adult cat's body never does: growing. That growth is fast and expensive. A kitten burns far more calories per kilogram of body weight than an adult cat, because it is building muscle, bone and brain tissue every single day. Its protein needs are higher, because protein is the building material for all of that new tissue. Its fat needs are higher too, because fat is the most concentrated source of the energy growth demands. And its mineral needs are different: a growing kitten needs the right balance of calcium and phosphorus to build strong bones, and getting that balance wrong in either direction can cause problems that last into adulthood.

Two nutrients deserve a special mention. DHA, an omega-3 fatty acid, supports the development of the brain and eyes, and good kitten foods include it — it is one of the clearest differences between a food made for growth and a food made for maintenance. Taurine, an amino acid cats cannot make enough of on their own, is essential for heart and eye health, and it must be present in the food in the right amount. Adult cat food contains taurine too, but in amounts matched to an adult's needs, not a kitten's. The practical point is simple: a food labelled complete and balanced for kittens or for growth has been formulated for exactly this job, and an adult food has not. Feed the food that matches the life stage.

What to Look For on the Label

The label is where a kitten food earns its name. Start with the life-stage statement: look for wording that says the food is complete and balanced for kittens, for growth, or for all life stages. "All life stages" means the food meets the needs of both kittens and adults, which is fine for a kitten, but a food made specifically for kittens is usually the better fit, because it is tuned to growth rather than a compromise between growth and maintenance. Avoid any food that says it is for adult maintenance only — that is the one label that tells you the food is not right for a kitten.

Next, read the ingredient list. Look for a named animal protein — chicken, turkey, salmon, lamb — in the first one or two positions. Cats are obligate carnivores: they need animal protein, and a named protein tells you what animal the food is made from. After the protein, look for a named fat source and recognisable ingredients. Then check the guaranteed analysis: kitten foods are usually higher in protein and fat than adult foods, and the label should show that clearly. The same label-reading habits apply if your kitten has a sensitive stomach — our guide to the best cat food for indoor cats explains how indoor formulas differ, and the label skills in our guide to choosing food for dogs with allergies apply just as well to reading a cat food label for hidden ingredients. Do not choose by the picture on the bag or by words like "premium" or "natural" — those are marketing, not nutrition. Read the life-stage statement, the ingredient list and the guaranteed analysis, in that order.

Weaning: From Milk to Solid Food

Weaning is the kitten's first food transition, and it should be gradual. Most kittens begin to show interest in solid food at around 3 to 4 weeks old, while they are still nursing. Offer a soft kitten food — wet kitten food, or dry kitten food softened with a little warm water or kitten milk replacer — in a shallow dish, and let the kitten lick and explore it. Do not force it: a kitten that is pushed will only learn to avoid the dish. By 5 to 6 weeks, most kittens are eating solid food regularly, and by 6 to 8 weeks they are usually fully weaned. A kitten should not leave its mother before 8 weeks, and nursing is about far more than food — the mother teaches the kitten how to eat, groom and play, and those lessons matter as much as the nutrition.

If you are hand-rearing an orphaned kitten, the rules are different: use a proper kitten milk replacer, never cow's milk, and follow your vet's guidance on feeding amounts and timing. Cow's milk is not a substitute for a mother's milk — it does not have the right balance of nutrients, and it commonly causes diarrhoea in kittens. If a kitten you have just brought home is younger than 8 weeks, talk to your vet straight away about feeding and care, because a very young kitten needs more than a bag of kitten food.

How Much to Feed, and How Often

A kitten's stomach is small and its energy needs are high, so the answer is little and often. As a guide, feed 4 meals a day up to about 6 months old, then 3 meals a day until around 12 months. Start with the feeding guide on the food packaging for your kitten's age and weight, and adjust by body condition: you should be able to feel the ribs easily under a thin layer of fat, and see a waist when you look down from above. Young kittens eat a surprising amount for their size, and their appetite grows fast as they grow — so weigh your kitten weekly and check the guide again as the numbers change. A kitten that is growing well, playing hard and producing normal stools is being fed about right; a kitten that is thin, or one that is gaining weight too fast, needs the amount adjusted and a vet's opinion.

Measure the food rather than guessing. A kitchen scale or a measuring cup takes seconds, and it stops the slow drift that turns a healthy kitten into an overweight young cat. Treats count too: keep them small, keep them to a small share of the daily calories, and use them for play and training rather than as extra meals. And always leave fresh water available. Kittens that eat mainly dry food need to drink, and a water bowl kept clean and filled — away from the food bowl, which many cats prefer — helps them do it.

Wet Food, Dry Food, or Both?

Both wet and dry kitten food can work well, and many owners feed a mix. Wet food is soft, easy for a young kitten to eat, and adds a lot of water to the diet, which supports hydration — but it spoils once opened and is more expensive per calorie. Dry food is convenient, easy to measure, and good for free-feeding a small amount between meals if your vet is happy with that — but it is hard, and a very young kitten may struggle with it until its teeth are in. A common approach is wet food for the main meals with a little dry food available, or wet food with dry food mixed in. Soaking dry kitten food in warm water for a few minutes is another simple way to soften it for a young kitten, and it releases more smell, which helps a kitten that is unsure about a new food. Whichever you choose, make sure the food is labelled for kittens — the wet-or-dry question is a preference, but the life-stage label is not.

Fussy Eaters and Sensitive Stomachs

Some kittens are fussy, and some have stomachs that object to every change. A kitten that will not eat a new food is usually not being difficult — it is being cautious, and that caution is normal. Try warming the food slightly, which releases more smell, or adding a spoonful of warm water. Offer the food in a quiet place, away from the litter tray and away from other pets, and give the kitten time. Do not offer a different food every time one is refused: a kitten that learns that refusing food brings something new will keep refusing, and a kitten that is switched constantly will never settle on a diet.

A sensitive stomach is different from fussiness. If a kitten has soft stools or an upset stomach after a food change, go back a step: feed the food it was eating before, then reintroduce the new food more slowly, over 10 to 14 days instead of 7. A few soft stools during a switch are common and usually settle. Persistent diarrhoea, vomiting, blood in the stool, or a kitten that stops eating or drinking is a vet visit, not a food experiment — kittens dehydrate fast, and a kitten that is not eating needs to be seen quickly. If your kitten has a food sensitivity, your vet may suggest a limited-ingredient or hypoallergenic kitten food, and the label-reading approach in our guide to choosing food for dogs with allergies is a useful model for how to read those labels carefully.

Foods to Avoid

The dangerous foods for cats do not become safe for kittens — and a kitten is more vulnerable to them because of its small size and smaller reserves. Keep these well out of reach:

  • Cow's milk — most cats, including kittens, are lactose intolerant. It commonly causes diarrhoea, and it is not a substitute for a mother's milk or a kitten milk replacer.
  • Onions and garlic — in any form, they can damage red blood cells and cause anaemia.
  • Chocolate — contains theobromine, which cats cannot process properly. Dark chocolate and baking chocolate are the most dangerous.
  • Grapes and raisins — can cause kidney problems in cats, and there is no safe amount.
  • Xylitol (birch sugar) — a sweetener in some sugar-free gum, sweets and peanut butter. It is dangerous for pets.
  • Raw fish and raw meat — can carry bacteria and parasites, and raw fish contains an enzyme that destroys thiamine, a vitamin kittens need.
  • Cooked bones — they splinter easily and can injure the mouth, throat or intestines.
  • Dog food — dog food does not contain the taurine and other nutrients cats need. A kitten that eats dog food regularly will become deficient.

If your kitten eats any of these, call your vet or an animal poison helpline right away rather than waiting for symptoms — early advice makes a real difference, and a small kitten has less room for error than an adult cat.

Switching to Adult Cat Food

Kitten food is a first-year food, and the switch to adult food comes when growth has mostly finished — for most cats, at around 12 months old. Larger breeds, such as Maine Coons, grow more slowly and may stay on kitten food until 15 to 18 months, so ask your vet if your kitten is a large breed. Switch gradually over 7 to 10 days: start with about a quarter of the adult food mixed into three quarters of the kitten food, move to half and half, then three quarters adult, then fully adult — watching appetite, energy and stools at each step. If your kitten is still underweight, was ill, or is a large breed still growing, your vet may suggest staying on kitten food a little longer.

After the switch, the feeding pattern changes too: an adult cat usually eats 2 meals a day, and its calorie needs are lower than a kitten's, so the portions that were right for a growing kitten will be too much for an adult. Watch the waistline in the first months after the switch — weight gain after neutering is common, and it is easier to prevent than to reverse. For what changes in the other direction, at the other end of a cat's life, see our senior cat care checklist, which covers food, litter tray, and vet visits for an older cat. And if you also share your home with a dog, remember the two species eat very differently: our guide to the best dog food for puppies explains what a growing dog needs, and it is not what your kitten needs.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Feeding adult cat food to a kitten — adult food is made for a cat that has stopped growing, and it is short on the calories, protein and minerals a kitten needs.
  • Feeding dog food to a kitten — dog food lacks the taurine and other nutrients cats need.
  • Offering cow's milk — most kittens are lactose intolerant, and milk causes diarrhoea rather than helping them grow.
  • Switching foods overnight — a kitten's gut needs a gradual 7 to 10 day change, and a sudden switch usually means an upset stomach.
  • Free-feeding without measuring — it is easy to overfeed a kitten that always has food available, and the extra weight is hard to lose later.
  • Choosing food by the picture on the bag — read the life-stage statement, the ingredient list and the guaranteed analysis instead.
  • Ignoring a kitten that will not eat — a kitten that stops eating or drinking needs a vet visit quickly, not a different bag of food.
  • Skipping vet checks and vaccinations — food is only part of a kitten's first year; the vet visits, vaccinations and worming in our puppy vaccination schedule post are written for dogs, but the principle is the same for kittens: ask your vet for the kitten schedule and keep it.

The Bottom Line

The best cat food for kittens is a complete and balanced food labelled for kittens or for growth, with a named animal protein first, enough calories, protein and fat to support fast growth, DHA for brain and eye development, and the right balance of calcium and phosphorus for growing bones — fed in 4 small meals a day up to 6 months, then 3 meals a day until around 12 months. Start solid food at 3 to 4 weeks, wean fully by 8 weeks, measure every meal, weigh your kitten weekly, and switch to adult food gradually at around 12 months. If your kitten is losing weight, refusing food, or has persistent diarrhoea, see your vet before changing foods. For more on your cat's life stages, see our best cat food for indoor cats guide for the adult years, and our senior cat care checklist for the later years — and the rest of our Health & Tips guides. And remember: this article is general guidance only. If your kitten is not eating, not drinking, or showing any sign of illness, ask your vet before changing their diet or routine.

Frequently asked questions

When should a kitten start eating solid food?

Most kittens begin to show interest in solid food at around 3 to 4 weeks old, while still nursing. Offer a soft kitten food mixed with a little warm water or kitten milk replacer, and let the kitten lick and explore it. By 6 to 8 weeks, most kittens are eating mainly solid food, and by 8 weeks they are usually fully weaned. Never take a kitten from its mother before 8 weeks just to start solid food — nursing and the mother's care matter for more than nutrition.

Can kittens eat adult cat food?

Kittens should not eat adult cat food as their main diet. Adult food is made for a cat that has stopped growing, so it has fewer calories and lower levels of the protein, fat and minerals a growing kitten needs. A kitten fed only adult food can grow too slowly and miss the nutrients its bones, brain and immune system need. Feed a food labelled complete and balanced for kittens or for growth, and keep it up until around 12 months of age.

How much should I feed my kitten?

Kittens need several small meals a day: 4 meals a day up to about 6 months old, then 3 meals a day until around 12 months. Start with the feeding guide on the food packaging for your kitten's age and weight, and adjust by body condition — you should be able to feel the ribs easily under a thin layer of fat. Young kittens eat a lot for their size, and their appetite grows fast, so weigh your kitten weekly and check the guide again as it grows.

When should I switch my kitten to adult cat food?

Most kittens switch to adult food at around 12 months old, when their growth has mostly finished. Larger breeds, such as Maine Coons, grow more slowly and may stay on kitten food until 15 to 18 months — ask your vet if you are unsure. Switch gradually over 7 to 10 days, mixing a little more adult food into the kitten food each day, and watch appetite, energy and stools at each step. If your kitten is still underweight or was ill, your vet may suggest staying on kitten food a little longer.
