Cat Vaccination Schedule: Kittens & Adult Cats Guide

November 25, 2026 • 15 min read • PawSmartCentral

A cat vaccination schedule is a plan, not a single appointment. Kittens usually need a series of visits in their first months, adult cats need boosters at intervals a vet sets for each vaccine, and the exact plan depends on the cat's age, health, history, lifestyle, and the rules where you live. This guide explains that plan in plain language: what the commonly used kitten ages mean, what core and lifestyle vaccines are, how adult boosters work, what happens at a vaccination visit, what side effects owners commonly notice, what to do when a shot is missed, and which questions to ask your vet. It is general information only, not veterinary advice for an individual cat, and it does not replace a vet's judgement. Cats are not small dogs, and a cat's schedule is not a copy of a dog's schedule, although the idea of a series followed by boosters is similar. Our puppy vaccination schedule covers dogs separately, and this guide links to it rather than repeating it. For the feeding side of kitten care, see our guide to the best cat food for kittens, and for older cats, our senior cat care checklist. Vaccination, feeding, and everyday care work together, but each needs its own plan, and the vaccination plan should be agreed with your vet.

Why Kittens Need a Series, Not a Single Shot

Kittens are born with a form of temporary protection passed from their mother, mainly through her first milk. That protection, often called maternal antibody protection, helps a very young kitten, but it fades at different times in different kittens, commonly over the first weeks and months of life. While it is present, it can also make a vaccine less effective, because the maternal protection may neutralise the vaccine before the kitten's own immune system has learned from it. That is why vets commonly give a series of doses spaced a few weeks apart, rather than relying on one injection. The aim of the series is that at least one dose lands after the maternal protection has faded enough for the kitten's own immune system to respond and remember. A kitten that receives only one early dose may therefore not be reliably protected, even if the visit is recorded as a vaccination. The series is usually completed at around 16 weeks of age, and it is followed by a booster at around one year of age, which helps to lock in the protection from the kitten series. After that, the adult pattern is a set of boosters at longer intervals, decided vaccine by vaccine.

Understanding that logic helps owners to make sense of advice that can otherwise sound like repetition for its own sake. Each visit in the series is not simply the same shot given again because the previous one was wasted. Each visit is a timed attempt, and the timing matters because the window when maternal protection fades is not the same for every kitten. A kitten from a well-vaccinated mother, a kitten that nursed well, and a kitten that was orphaned and hand-reared may start from different levels of maternal protection, and a vet will take the kitten's history into account. The same logic explains why a vet asks about the kitten's background at the first visit, why records from a breeder or shelter matter, and why a kitten adopted with no history may be given a different starting plan from a kitten whose earlier doses are documented. It also explains why socialisation and protection have to be balanced in the early weeks, as discussed later in this guide. A kitten needs new experiences while it is young, and it also needs to avoid high-risk exposure until its series is further along. Your vet can help you to find the safe middle ground for your home, rather than leaving you to guess.

It is also worth separating two ideas that owners often merge: being vaccinated, and being protected. A record that says a vaccine was given is not the same as a guarantee that the individual cat is protected against the disease. Vaccines greatly reduce the risk of serious disease in most cats, and they are widely regarded by vets as one of the most important preventive measures in cat care, but no vaccine is described in this guide as providing complete protection for every cat. Health, age, stress, other illness, medicine, and the timing of doses can all affect the response. That is why boosters matter, why a vet examines the cat before vaccinating, and why a cat that is unwell on the day of a planned vaccination may have the visit rearranged. A vaccination visit is a health check as well as an injection, and the examination is part of the value of the visit, not a delay to it.

Core Vaccines and Lifestyle Vaccines: The Usual Distinction

Vets commonly divide cat vaccines into core vaccines, which are recommended for most cats, and non-core or lifestyle vaccines, which are recommended depending on risk. The core group commonly discussed for cats is the combination vaccine often shortened to FVRCP, which covers feline viral rhinotracheitis, feline calicivirus, and feline panleukopenia. Those three names describe serious respiratory and systemic illnesses of cats, and panleukopenia in particular can be severe in kittens. Rabies vaccination is also core in many places, and in some places it is legally required, so the legal position where you live is part of the decision, not an optional extra. Your vet will tell you whether rabies vaccination is required or recommended for your cat, and at what age, because the rules and the timing vary by country and region. Do not assume that the rule in one place applies in another, and do not assume that an indoor cat is automatically exempt from a legal requirement.

Lifestyle vaccines are the vaccines a vet discusses after asking how the cat lives. The best-known example is the vaccine against feline leukaemia virus, often shortened to FeLV. It is commonly discussed for kittens and for cats that go outside, meet unfamiliar cats, share a home with a cat whose status is unknown, or live in a multi-cat household where cats groom and share bowls. A strictly indoor cat that never meets another cat may be assessed differently, but that assessment is for the vet, based on the real household, including future plans such as fostering, a new cat joining the home, or a move to a home with outdoor access. Other vaccines may be discussed in particular regions or circumstances, such as vaccines against feline immunodeficiency virus or against certain respiratory bacteria, and their availability and use vary a great deal by place. The useful question to ask is not whether a vaccine exists, but whether it is relevant to this cat, in this home, in this area, at this age. A good vet will explain the reasoning, including any downsides, so that you can make an informed choice together.

Combination products add a further wrinkle. Many kitten vaccines are given as a single combined injection that covers several diseases at once, and the brand, the combination, and the number of diseases covered can differ. That is normal, and it is not a reason to compare one clinic's plan with another's line by line and conclude that one must be wrong. What matters is the record: which diseases were covered, which product was used, when it was given, and when the next dose is due. Keep that record, ask for a copy at each visit, and take a photograph of it for your phone as a backup. If you change vet, the new vet needs that record to continue the series correctly, and without it the new vet may have to assume the worst and restart part of the plan. A clear record protects the cat from both gaps and unnecessary repetition, and it is needed for boarding, travel, grooming, and rehoming, where proof of vaccination is commonly requested.

The Kitten Schedule: The Ages Vets Commonly Use

The ages below are the pattern vets commonly follow for kittens with no known vaccine history, given as a guide to what to expect. They are not a prescription for your kitten, and your vet may adjust them for the kitten's age at adoption, health, history, or local guidance. Use the pattern to plan visits and to understand what each visit is for, and let the vet set the actual dates.

  • Around 6 to 8 weeks: first core vaccination visit, commonly the FVRCP combination, plus a full health check, a weight check, and a discussion of feeding, flea and worm treatment, litter, and socialisation. The vet will ask about the kitten's background and any records from the breeder or shelter.
  • Around 9 to 12 weeks: second core dose, commonly FVRCP again, with a health check. A lifestyle vaccine, such as FeLV, may be started at this stage if the vet recommends it for the kitten, and the vet may discuss microchipping and neutering plans.
  • Around 12 to 16 weeks: further core dose or doses, commonly completing the kitten FVRCP series, and a second FeLV dose if that vaccine was started. Rabies vaccination is commonly given at around 12 to 16 weeks where it is required or recommended, subject to local rules.
  • Around one year of age: booster visit, commonly covering the core vaccines and any lifestyle vaccine the cat is receiving, plus a full adult health check. This is the visit that moves the cat from the kitten plan to the adult plan.

Between those headline visits, a vet may ask to see a kitten more often, for example for a weight check, a second health check, or a flea and worm treatment, and those extra visits are not a sign that something is wrong. Kittens grow fast, and a small kitten can change a great deal in two weeks. A vet who sees the kitten regularly can spot a slow weight gain, a developing eye or breathing problem, or a feeding issue early, when it is easiest to help. Take the kitten's record to every visit, including any treatment given at home, and tell the vet about any change since the last visit, however small it seems: a softer stool, a sneeze, a change in appetite, or a new behaviour. Kittens cannot describe how they feel, and the owner's observations are the vet's best early warning system. If the kitten is unwell on the day of a planned vaccination, call the practice before the visit. The vet may still want to see the kitten, or may rearrange the vaccination until the kitten is well, and that decision is a clinical one, not a matter of convenience.

A kitten adopted at an older age, for example at 12 or 16 weeks, does not simply skip the earlier steps. The vet will usually start the series at the first visit and space the doses from there, which may mean the series finishes later than it would for a kitten adopted at eight weeks. A kitten adopted with a partial record, where one or two doses are documented, will usually have the series continued from the documented point, with the vet checking that the intervals and the products make sense. A kitten with no record at all is usually treated as unvaccinated, which is the safe assumption, and the series is started from the beginning. In each case, the principle is the same: the vet builds the plan from the evidence available, and the owner provides the evidence by bringing every record, however incomplete, to the first visit. Do not guess at dates, and do not fill in a missing record from memory if you are unsure. Say what you know, say what you do not know, and let the vet decide.

Adult Cats: Boosters, Intervals, and Why They Differ

Adult vaccination is less about a single schedule and more about a set of intervals, one for each vaccine. After the one-year booster, a vet commonly sets the next due dates by vaccine, by the product used, by the cat's health and lifestyle, and by local rules. Some vaccines are commonly boosted every year, others every three years, and the pattern can change if the cat's lifestyle changes, if a new vaccine product is used, or if guidance changes. That is why an adult cat may be due for one vaccine at a visit and not for another, and why a neighbour's cat may be on a different pattern from yours without either plan being wrong. The question to ask at each adult visit is simple: which vaccines were given today, which were not, and when is each one next due? Ask for the dates to be written down, and keep them with the record. A calendar reminder, set on the day of the visit, is the simplest way to avoid a missed booster, and it is more reliable than relying on memory or on a reminder card that may not arrive.

Adult health checks matter as much as the injections themselves. An annual or twice-yearly health check for an adult cat, and more frequent checks for a senior cat, gives the vet a chance to weigh the cat, examine teeth, eyes, ears, skin, and coat, feel the abdomen, listen to the heart and lungs, and discuss appetite, drinking, toileting, activity, and behaviour. Many cat health problems, including dental disease, kidney problems, thyroid problems, and weight gain, develop slowly, and an owner who sees the cat every day may miss a gradual change that a vet notices at once. Our senior cat care checklist explains the older-cat side of that care in detail, and our guide to keeping a cat happy indoors covers the everyday environment that supports a healthy adult cat. Vaccination visits are a good moment to raise any concern, from a change in drinking to a new lump, because the cat is already at the practice and the vet can examine it there and then. Do not wait for a vaccination visit to report an urgent problem, however. A cat that stops eating, struggles to breathe, cannot pass urine, or shows sudden weakness needs urgent veterinary attention, whether or not a vaccination is due.

Lifestyle changes should trigger a vaccination review, even if no booster is due. A strictly indoor cat that begins to go outside, a cat that starts to visit a cattery or a groomer, a new cat joining the household, a move to a new area, or a plan to travel with the cat can all change the risk picture. Tell the vet about the change before it happens, if you can, so that any vaccine that needs time to take effect can be given in good time. Boarding catteries and pet travel schemes commonly require proof of vaccination, sometimes with a minimum interval between the last vaccination and the stay or the journey, and leaving that check until the week before travel can cause a problem that could have been avoided. The same applies to a cat that is to be bred, a cat that is pregnant, and a cat that is receiving medicine that affects the immune system. In each case, the vet's advice takes priority over any general schedule, including this guide, because the individual circumstances change the risk and the timing.

Indoor Cats, Outdoor Cats, and the Exposure Question

Owners of indoor cats sometimes ask whether vaccination is needed at all, and the short answer most vets give is that core vaccination is still recommended. An indoor cat is not sealed off from the world. People come and go, shoes and clothing can carry material from outside, windows and balconies are open in warm weather, other pets visit, and the cat itself visits the vet, where other animals are present. A new cat joining the home, a foster cat, or a visiting cat can also introduce exposure that an indoor cat would not otherwise meet. None of that means an indoor cat faces the same risk as a roaming cat, and the vet's plan for an indoor cat may properly differ in the lifestyle vaccines, while the core vaccines remain. The decision should be based on the cat's real life, described honestly, including the exceptions: the cat that is indoor except for supervised garden time, the cat that escapes occasionally, the cat that meets the neighbour's cat through a fence, and the cat that will be boarded while the owner is away.

Outdoor and roaming cats face a wider set of exposures, and the vet's discussion will usually reflect that. Contact with unfamiliar cats, hunting, fighting, and roaming through areas used by other animals all increase the chance of meeting infectious diseases, and a roaming cat may also need more frequent flea, tick, and worm treatment, which the vet will discuss alongside vaccination. A roaming cat that comes home with a bite wound, an abscess, or a sudden illness should be seen promptly, and the vet should be told about the cat's vaccination status and about any bite or fight, because that history changes the assessment. Neutering, microchipping, and keeping a roaming cat in at night are separate decisions from vaccination, but they are part of the same conversation about a safe outdoor life, and the vet can advise on all of them together. For the home environment, the indoor-cat guide linked above is a useful companion, whether the cat is indoor, outdoor, or a mixture, because enrichment, safe resting places, and a predictable routine support health at every age.

Multi-cat households need a household plan, not just an individual plan. When a new cat joins a home, the vet will commonly advise a health check, a quarantine or separation period, and a vaccination and testing plan before the new cat meets the resident cats, especially if the new cat's history is unknown. That advice protects both the new cat and the residents, and it should be followed even if the new cat looks healthy, because some infections spread before signs appear. In a multi-cat home, keep a separate record for each cat, with its own due dates, because cats in the same home are often on different plans, and a shared record is easy to confuse. Label the records, keep them in the same folder, and take the right record to each cat's visit. If one cat in the home becomes ill, tell the vet about the other cats, their vaccination status, and whether they share bowls, litter trays, and sleeping places, because that information helps the vet to advise on the whole household, not just the sick cat.

Before the First Visit: Records, Carrier, and Questions

A good first vaccination visit starts before you leave home. Gather every record you have: the breeder or shelter paperwork, any vaccination card, any treatment given, the date the kitten arrived, and the name and contact details of the previous vet, if there was one. Note the kitten's food, including the brand and the amount, as our kitten food guide explains, because diet and weight are part of the health check. Note any flea or worm treatment already given, with the product name and date, so the vet does not repeat a treatment unnecessarily. Note any signs you have seen, however minor, and any questions you want to ask, written down, because it is easy to forget a question in the consulting room. A short written list, in the order that matters to you, is the most reliable way to leave the visit with answers.

Prepare the carrier as well as the paperwork. A secure, covered carrier, with a familiar blanket and, if the vet advises it, a calming spray used in advance, makes the journey safer and less stressful for the cat. Do not carry a cat loose in a car, and do not open the carrier in the waiting room, where dogs and other cats add stress and exposure. Keep the carrier covered, keep the cat inside, and tell the receptionist if the cat is unwell, nervous, or likely to be a risk to other animals, so that you can wait in a suitable place. If the cat is a rescue that has never travelled, a few short practice sessions with the carrier at home, with treats and the door open, can help, and the vet can advise on reducing travel stress. Arrive a little early, bring the record, and be ready to answer questions about the cat's appetite, drinking, toileting, activity, and behaviour since arrival. Those answers are part of the examination, and an honest "I am not sure" is more useful than a guess.

At the visit, ask the vet to explain the plan in plain terms, and to write it down. Useful questions include: which vaccines are being given today, and which diseases do they cover? Which vaccines are lifestyle vaccines, and why are they, or are they not, recommended for this cat? When is the next dose due, for each vaccine? What side effects should I watch for, and what should I do if I see them? When can the kitten safely meet other cats, go outside, or be boarded? What flea and worm treatment is recommended, and when is it next due? Is there anything about this cat's weight, teeth, or general health that I should watch? And what is the cost of the plan, including the booster visits, so that I can budget for it? A vet who explains the plan, and who writes down the dates, is giving you the tools to follow it. If anything is unclear, say so at the visit, not afterwards. There is no benefit in leaving with a plan you do not understand, and vets expect owners to ask questions.

What Happens at a Vaccination Visit

A vaccination visit usually follows a predictable order, and knowing it in advance makes the visit less worrying. The vet or nurse takes a history, asking about the cat's background, appetite, drinking, toileting, activity, and any treatment or illness since the last visit. The vet then examines the cat: weight, temperature if needed, eyes, ears, mouth and teeth, skin and coat, heart and lungs, abdomen, and general condition. Only then, if the cat is well enough, is the vaccine given, usually as a small injection under the skin, sometimes in two sites if two products are used. The injection itself is brief, and most cats react with a flinch or a brief protest, then settle. The vet will record the vaccine, the batch details, and the next due date, and will give you the updated record and any aftercare advice. The whole visit is usually short, and the examination is the part that takes the time, which is as it should be.

After the injection, the vet may ask you to wait a short while, or may advise you to watch the cat closely for the rest of the day, in case of an immediate reaction. Keep the cat indoors, quiet, and warm for the rest of the day, offer water and a small meal, and avoid a big play session, a bath, or a stressful journey. A kitten that is sleepy, a little off its food, or tender where the injection was given is showing a commonly reported response, as discussed in the next section, and usually recovers within a day. Keep the record safe, set the reminder for the next visit, and note any reaction, however mild, to tell the vet about at the next visit. If the cat is due a flea or worm treatment, the vet may give it at the same visit or advise a different day, and the advice on timing should be followed, because some treatments should not be given on the same day as a vaccine. Follow the vet's instructions for your cat, rather than a general rule from another source.

Side Effects: What Is Common, and When to Call the Vet

Most cats have no noticeable reaction to vaccination, or only a mild, short-lived one. Commonly reported mild responses include tenderness or a small, soft swelling where the injection was given, mild tiredness, a slightly reduced appetite, and a low-grade temperature for a day. Those responses usually settle within 24 to 48 hours without treatment, and the cat can be kept comfortable with rest, warmth, water, and a quiet place to sleep. A small lump at the injection site is commonly reported, and it usually disappears within a few days to a few weeks, but it should be mentioned to the vet, especially if it grows, becomes painful, or is still present after a few weeks. Keep a note of the date, the site, and the size, and tell the vet at the next visit, or sooner if you are worried. Do not give a cat human pain medicine, or any medicine not prescribed by the vet, because many human medicines are unsafe for cats.

Serious reactions are uncommon, but owners should know the signs and act quickly if they appear. Swelling of the face, muzzle, or eyes, repeated vomiting, difficulty breathing, weakness, collapse, or a severe reaction within hours of the injection are emergencies, and the cat should be taken to a vet or an emergency clinic immediately, with a phone call on the way if possible. Tell the practice which vaccine was given, when, and what signs you have seen. A cat that remains unwell for more than a day or two after vaccination, that stops eating or drinking, that develops a fever, or that shows any new sign that worries you should also be seen, rather than watched at home on the assumption that it must be a vaccine reaction. A vaccination visit does not make a cat immune to other illness, and a new illness can start at the same time as a vaccination by coincidence. When in doubt, call the practice. A phone call costs nothing, and a vet or nurse can advise whether the cat needs to be seen, and how urgently.

Missed, Late, and Unknown-History Vaccinations

Vaccinations are missed for ordinary reasons: a holiday, a move, an illness, a lost record, a rescue with no history, or simply a date that slipped. The response is the same in every case: contact the vet, explain what happened, and let the vet set the corrected plan. A short delay of a few days or a week commonly means the next dose is given late, and the series continues. A longer gap may mean the vet recommends restarting part of the series, or giving a booster and then checking the response, depending on the vaccine, the gap, and the cat's history. A cat with no record is commonly treated as unvaccinated, and the series is started from the beginning, which is the safe course. Do not try to catch up by giving a vaccine at home, by buying a vaccine online, or by giving an extra dose, because storage, dose, timing, and technique all affect whether a vaccine works, and a badly stored or badly given vaccine may give a false sense of protection.

If a cat misses a vaccination and then meets a risk in the meantime, for example a fight with an unknown cat, a bite, or exposure to a sick cat, tell the vet about the exposure as well as the missed dose, because the advice may change. The vet may want to see the cat sooner, may advise a period of observation, or may adjust the plan. The same applies if the cat becomes ill while a vaccination is overdue: the illness should be assessed on its own, and the vaccination plan should be reset once the cat is well. Keep the record up to date through every change, and ask for the revised dates in writing. A missed vaccination is a common, fixable problem, and vets deal with it every day. The problem becomes serious only when it is hidden, delayed, or dealt with at home, and the cat is left with a gap in protection that nobody has planned for.

Costs, Packages, and Planning Ahead

The cost of a cat vaccination plan varies by country, region, practice, and the vaccines included, and this guide deliberately gives no price figures, because a figure from one place would mislead a reader in another. Ask the practice for the full cost of the kitten series, including the one-year booster, at the first visit, and ask what is included: the examination, the vaccines, the record, and any flea or worm treatment, or whether those are charged separately. Many practices offer a kitten package, a vaccination plan, or a monthly payment plan that spreads the cost of preventive care, and it is worth asking what is available, what it covers, and what it does not cover, before deciding. A package that covers the kitten series but not the adult boosters, or that covers vaccination but not neutering, microchipping, or flea treatment, may still be good value, but the owner should know what is included.

Budgeting for vaccination is part of budgeting for a cat, alongside food, litter, insurance, and routine care. Our kitten food guide and our indoor cat food guide cover the feeding side of that budget, and the vaccination plan should be budgeted in the same way: as a regular, predictable cost, not as an emergency. If cost is a barrier, ask the practice about options, ask local animal charities and shelters about low-cost vaccination days, and ask about any scheme in your area, rather than skipping vaccination. A skipped series is not a saving, because the diseases vaccination prevents are costly and distressing to treat, and some are fatal. A vet or a charity that knows cost is a concern can often help with a plan, a payment arrangement, or a referral, and asking is always better than doing nothing.

Records, Boarding, Travel, and Proof of Vaccination

A vaccination record is a practical document, not just a keepsake. Boarding catteries, groomers, pet sitters, breeders, shelters, and travel schemes commonly ask for proof of vaccination, and some require it a minimum time before the stay or journey. Keep the original record in a safe place, keep a photograph of it on your phone, and keep a note of the next due dates where you will see them. If you move, change vet, or rehome a cat, pass the record on, and ask the new vet to confirm that the record is complete and that the plan is understood. If a record is lost, the vet practice will usually have a copy, and can issue a replacement, although a lost record from a previous practice may take time to obtain. Start that request early, not the week before a cattery stay.

Travel with a cat adds a further layer of requirements, which vary by destination and by mode of transport, and which can include vaccination, microchipping, parasite treatment, and paperwork, sometimes with strict timing. Check the requirements for the destination well in advance, from an official source, and discuss them with the vet in good time, because some requirements need weeks or months to complete. Do not rely on a general guide, including this one, for travel requirements, and do not assume that the requirements for one country apply to another. The same caution applies to boarding: check the cattery's requirements before booking, confirm that the cat's vaccinations will be in date for the whole stay, and book the booster visit in good time if one will fall due during the stay. A little advance planning avoids a cancelled booking, a delayed journey, or a cat that cannot be boarded when the owner needs to travel.

Common Mistakes Owners Make

  • Assuming one kitten visit is enough. The kitten series needs several doses, spaced weeks apart, and a single visit does not reliably complete it.
  • Copying a dog's schedule for a cat. Cats and dogs have different vaccines, different diseases, and different timings. Follow the cat plan your vet sets, and see our puppy schedule only for dogs.
  • Skipping core vaccines because the cat is indoor. Indoor cats still face exposure, and core vaccination is commonly recommended for them. Discuss lifestyle vaccines with your vet, rather than skipping vaccination altogether.
  • Delaying the first visit because the kitten looks healthy. A healthy-looking kitten still needs a health check and a planned series, and the first visit is the time to set the plan.
  • Losing the record. Without a record, a new vet must assume the cat is unvaccinated, and boarding or travel proof becomes difficult. Keep the record, photograph it, and take it to every visit.
  • Trying to catch up at home. Vaccines bought online or given at home may be badly stored, wrongly dosed, or wrongly timed. Leave vaccination to the vet.
  • Ignoring a missed date. A missed booster is common and fixable if the vet is told promptly. A missed booster that is hidden leaves a gap in protection.
  • Exposing a kitten too soon. Meeting unfamiliar cats, visiting high-risk places, or going outside before the series is far enough along can expose a kitten before it is protected. Ask your vet when each step is safe.
  • Giving human medicine for a mild reaction. Many human medicines are unsafe for cats. Call the vet for advice, and give only medicine the vet prescribes.
  • Forgetting the adult boosters. The kitten series is only the start. Adult boosters, at the intervals the vet sets, keep the protection going for life.

The Bottom Line

A cat vaccination schedule is a lifetime plan that starts with a kitten series, commonly at around 6 to 8 weeks, with further doses every few weeks until about 16 weeks, followed by a booster at around one year, and then by adult boosters at intervals your vet sets for each vaccine. Core vaccines, commonly the FVRCP combination and rabies where required, are recommended for most cats, including indoor cats, and lifestyle vaccines, such as FeLV, are discussed according to how the cat lives. The vet's examination, the written record, and the next due dates are as important as the injections themselves, and the owner's job is to bring the record, describe the cat's real life honestly, keep the appointments, and call the practice promptly if a date is missed or the cat is unwell. Ask questions, keep the record safe, set reminders for the next dates, and review the plan whenever the cat's lifestyle changes. This guide is general information only. Always ask your vet about your cat's vaccination schedule, because your cat's age, health, history, lifestyle, and local rules all shape the plan.

Frequently asked questions

When should a kitten get its first vaccinations?

Kittens commonly start their core vaccinations at around 6 to 8 weeks of age, with further doses every 3 to 4 weeks until they are about 16 weeks old, but the exact timing depends on the kitten, its history, and local guidance, so your vet should set the dates. Bring any records from the breeder, shelter, or previous owner to the first visit, and ask the vet to confirm the plan in writing. Do not assume a kitten is protected after a single visit, and do not delay the first appointment because the kitten looks healthy.

Do indoor cats need vaccinations?

Most vets recommend core vaccinations for indoor cats as well as for cats that go outside, because indoor cats can still be exposed through an open window, a visitor's shoes or clothing, a new pet joining the home, or a trip to the vet, cattery, or groomer. The difference is usually in the additional, lifestyle vaccines, which are more often discussed for cats that roam, hunt, or meet unfamiliar cats. Ask your vet which vaccines suit your cat's actual lifestyle, rather than deciding on indoor status alone, and check any local legal requirements for rabies vaccination.

How often do adult cats need booster vaccinations?

After the kitten series, most cats receive a booster at around one year of age, and then further boosters at intervals your vet sets, often every one to three years depending on the vaccine, the cat's health, its lifestyle, and local rules. The interval is not the same for every vaccine, so an adult cat may be due for one vaccine and not for another at the same visit. Keep the vaccination record, ask the vet to write down the next due dates at each visit, and book the next appointment before you leave the practice.

What should I do if my cat misses a vaccination?

Contact your vet as soon as you realise a vaccination is overdue, and do not try to catch up at home or buy a vaccine to give yourself. A short delay often means the next dose is simply given late, while a longer gap may mean the vet recommends restarting part of the series or running extra checks first, depending on the vaccine and how much time has passed. Take the vaccination record to the appointment, tell the vet about any illness or medicine since the last visit, and ask for the revised dates in writing.
