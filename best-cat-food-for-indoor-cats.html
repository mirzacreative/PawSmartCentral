Best Cat Food for Indoor Cats: What to Feed

November 17, 2026 • 8 min read • PawSmartCentral

An indoor cat lives a very different life from a cat that roams outside, and its food should reflect that. An outdoor cat burns energy hunting, patrolling and climbing. An indoor cat sleeps most of the day, plays in short bursts, and walks to the food bowl instead of across a garden. The result is predictable: indoor cats gain weight more easily, swallow more hair from grooming while they are bored, and drink less water than they should. The best cat food for indoor cats is not a magic recipe — it is a complete and balanced food that fits this quieter lifestyle: controlled calories, enough fibre for hairballs, good-quality protein, and ingredients your cat can actually digest. This guide explains what indoor cats need, how to read a cat food label, whether wet or dry food is better, how feeding changes with age, and how to switch foods without upsetting your cat's stomach.

How Indoor Cats Are Different

Before choosing a food, it helps to understand why an indoor cat's needs differ. The biggest difference is activity. A cat that stays inside moves far less than a roaming cat, so it burns fewer calories — but it often eats just as much, especially if food is left out all day. Over months, that small daily surplus turns into weight gain, and extra weight makes everything harder: joints ache, grooming gets difficult, and the risk of diabetes rises. The second difference is grooming. Indoor cats groom a lot, partly because they have little else to do, and every grooming session sends loose hair into the stomach. Most of it passes through, but some of it collects into hairballs, which the cat then coughs up — unpleasant for the cat and messy for you. The third difference is hydration. Cats are naturally poor drinkers, and indoor cats, with no puddles or garden to explore, may drink even less. Low water intake makes urine more concentrated, which is hard on the kidneys and the urinary tract over time. None of this means indoor life is bad for cats — it keeps them safe from traffic, fights and disease — but it does mean their food needs to do a little more work. What Indoor Cat Food Should Do

A good indoor cat food does four jobs at once. First, weight control: it should be moderately lower in calories than a standard adult food, or at least easy to portion, so a less active cat stays at a healthy weight. That usually means a moderate fat level rather than a very rich recipe, and a feeding guide on the pack that you actually follow — a measuring cup, not a guess. Second, hairball control: extra fibre, from ingredients like beet pulp or cellulose, helps swallowed hair move through the digestive tract instead of collecting in the stomach. Fibre does not stop hairballs completely, but it reduces how often they happen. Third, digestibility: highly digestible ingredients mean your cat absorbs more nutrition from less food, which produces smaller, firmer stools and less waste. Look for foods where the protein comes from named animal sources rather than vague by-products. Fourth, the right protein level: cats are obligate carnivores and need animal protein, but more protein is not automatically better. A healthy adult indoor cat does well on a food with a solid, moderate protein level from quality sources; very high-protein, very high-calorie foods are designed for active or growing cats and can push an indoor cat's weight up. If your cat has a medical condition, such as kidney disease, the protein and mineral levels matter even more — and that is a conversation for your vet, not a label. How to Read a Cat Food Label

The label tells you more than the picture on the front of the bag. Start with the ingredient list: ingredients are listed by weight before cooking, so the first few matter most. Look for a named animal protein — chicken, salmon, turkey, lamb — in the first one or two positions. Vague terms such as "meat meal" or "animal derivatives" tell you very little about what is actually inside. Next, look for the complete and balanced statement: a reputable food states that it is complete and balanced for a particular life stage, following recognised nutritional guidelines. That statement is the single most important thing on the label — without it, the food may not provide everything your cat needs. Then check the guaranteed analysis, which lists minimum protein and fat and maximum fibre and moisture. For an indoor cat, moderate fat and a fibre level on the higher side of normal are what you want to see. Also check the calorie content, usually given per cup or per 100 grams, so you can compare foods fairly — a food that looks cheap per bag may be expensive per calorie. Finally, read the feeding guide and treat it as a starting point, not a rule: it is written for an average cat, and your cat's age, weight and activity will shift the right amount up or down. The same label-reading habits apply to dog food, as our guide to choosing the right food for allergies shows — a named protein first, a short readable ingredient list, and a complete-and-balanced statement.

Wet Food vs Dry Food for Indoor Cats

Neither wet nor dry food is better on its own — they solve different problems, and many indoor cats do best on a mix of both. Wet food is roughly three-quarters water, so every meal adds hydration, which supports the kidneys and the urinary tract. It is also usually lower in calories per bite, because the water fills the cat up, and many cats find it more appealing — useful for fussy eaters and older cats. Its downsides are practical: it is more expensive per calorie, it spoils once opened, and it does nothing for the teeth. Dry food is convenient and economical, it keeps well in the bowl, and the crunch can help reduce tartar a little. Its downsides are that it is calorie-dense — a small extra scoop is a big extra meal — and it adds almost no water to the diet, so a cat that eats only dry food must drink well on its own, which many cats do not. A common approach that works well is wet food for the main meals and a measured amount of dry food for the rest of the day, or for overnight grazing. Whichever you choose, the portion matters more than the format: weigh or measure the food, follow the feeding guide as a starting point, and adjust by watching your cat's weight and body shape over a few weeks. If you can feel the ribs easily under a thin layer of fat and your cat has a visible waist from above, the portion is about right.

Feeding by Life Stage

An indoor kitten, an indoor adult and an indoor senior cat do not need the same food. Kittens (under 12 months) are growing fast and need a kitten food: higher in calories and protein, fed in several small meals a day. Do not put a kitten on an indoor adult formula — it will not support proper growth. Adults (roughly 1 to 7 years) are the cats this guide is mainly about: a complete adult indoor formula, fed in measured meals, with weight watched twice a year. Senior cats (around 11 years and older) need a more individual approach. Some seniors lose weight and need more calories, some gain weight and need fewer, and some develop kidney or thyroid problems that change the diet entirely. For older cats, the vet's bloodwork matters more than any bag of food, and the food should be chosen to match it. Whatever the age, one rule stays the same: change the food for a reason — a new life stage, a weight problem, a vet's advice — not because a new bag looked interesting. How Much to Feed an Indoor Cat

Most indoor cats are overfed, not underfed, and the cause is usually free feeding — a full bowl, topped up whenever it empties. A better approach is measured meals: two meals a day for most adults, using the feeding guide on the pack as a starting point and a kitchen scale or a measuring cup to get it right. Weigh your cat every month or two — a bathroom scale works if you weigh yourself holding the cat and then subtract your own weight — and write the number down. A slow upward trend is your cue to cut the portion by about ten percent and check again in a month. Treats count too: keep them to a small share of the daily calories, and count them as part of the total rather than on top of it. Play also helps: ten minutes of active play a day, with a wand toy or a puzzle feeder, burns a few calories and gives a bored indoor cat something to do besides eat and groom. A puzzle feeder is especially useful — it slows eating down, makes the cat work for its food, and turns a meal into the kind of activity an indoor cat otherwise misses. If your cat is already overweight, do not crash-diet it: rapid weight loss in cats can cause serious liver problems. Ask your vet for a safe target and a slow, steady plan.

How to Switch Foods Safely

Cats are creatures of habit, and a sudden food change is one of the fastest ways to cause vomiting, diarrhoea or a cat that simply refuses to eat. Switch gradually over 7 to 10 days. Start with about a quarter new food mixed into three quarters of the old food, and keep it there for two or three days. Then move to half and half for two or three days, then three quarters new food, then fully new. At each step, watch the appetite, the stools and the energy level. Soft stools for a day or two during a switch are common and usually settle on their own; persistent diarrhoea, vomiting or a cat that stops eating is a reason to slow down or go back a step. If your cat refuses the new food entirely, try warming it slightly, or mixing in a spoonful of wet food — and accept that some cats take longer than ten days, especially older ones. Never let a cat go without eating for more than a day: a cat that stops eating, particularly an overweight one, can develop liver problems quickly, and that is a vet visit, not a waiting game. The same gradual approach applies when switching between wet and dry food, or between brands of the same type.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Free feeding all day — a full bowl with no measuring is the fastest route to an overweight indoor cat.
  • Choosing food by the picture on the bag — marketing words like "premium" and "natural" are not regulated the way the ingredient list is; read the label instead.
  • Feeding dog food to a cat — dog food lacks the nutrients cats need, including taurine, and it is not safe as a cat's diet.
  • Too many treats — treats, table scraps and milk add calories fast, and they are not part of a balanced diet.
  • Switching foods suddenly — a new food overnight usually means an upset stomach and a cat that will not eat it.
  • Ignoring the water bowl — fresh water in several places, a wide shallow bowl, or a pet fountain all help an indoor cat drink more.
  • Feeding the same food for life — a kitten, an adult and a senior cat have different needs, and the food should change with them.
  • Skipping the vet — weight, appetite and thirst changes are medical signs first; check them with a vet before changing the diet.

The Bottom Line

The best cat food for indoor cats is a complete and balanced food with a named animal protein first, moderate fat, enough fibre for hairball control, and a calorie level that fits a quieter life — fed in measured meals, with fresh water always available, and adjusted as your cat ages. Wet food, dry food or a mix can all work; the portion and the quality of the recipe matter far more than the brand. Read the label, measure the food, weigh your cat regularly, and switch foods slowly. And remember that food is only part of the picture: play, grooming, fresh water and regular vet visits keep an indoor cat healthy just as much as what is in the bowl. If your cat is gaining weight, losing weight, drinking much more than usual, or refusing food, ask your vet before changing anything — those are medical signs, and the right food is the one that fits your cat's health, not the one with the best advertising.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best food for an indoor cat?

The best food for an indoor cat is a complete and balanced diet with a named animal protein first, moderate fat, enough fibre for hairball control, and a calorie level matched to a less active lifestyle. Wet food, dry food, or a mix can all work — what matters most is the portion size and the overall quality of the recipe, not the brand name on the bag.

Do indoor cats need special food?

Indoor cats do not need a different species of food, but they do benefit from a recipe made for their lifestyle: fewer calories because they move less, more fibre to help pass swallowed hair, and highly digestible ingredients so smaller meals still deliver the nutrients they need. A standard adult food fed in the right amount can work, but indoor formulas make portion control easier.

Is wet or dry food better for indoor cats?

Neither is better on its own. Wet food adds water to the diet, which supports hydration and kidney health, and it is usually lower in calories per bite. Dry food is convenient, keeps well, and helps some cats graze sensibly. Many owners feed a mix: wet food for the main meals and a measured amount of dry food for the rest of the day.

How do I switch my indoor cat to a new food?

Switch gradually over 7 to 10 days. Start with about a quarter new food mixed into three quarters of the old food, then move to half and half, then three quarters new, then fully new — watching appetite, stools and energy at each step. If your cat refuses the new food or develops digestive upset, slow down the change or go back a step, and ask your vet if the problem continues.
