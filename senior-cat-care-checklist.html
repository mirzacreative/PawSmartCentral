Senior Cat Care Checklist for Cats Aged 11+

November 14, 2026 • 6 min read • PawSmartCentral

A cat of eleven or older is a senior, and senior cats have different needs from the cat you brought home years ago. They sleep more, move a little slower, and hide discomfort well — cats are experts at pretending nothing is wrong. That is why a checklist helps: it turns vague worry into a short list of things to check, and it catches small changes early, when they are easiest to deal with. This guide covers the full senior cat care checklist — vet visits and bloodwork, kidney and thyroid warning signs, diet and hydration, joint comfort, litter-box access, grooming, and keeping routines stable — so you can give an older cat a comfortable, healthy later life.

Vet Visits and Bloodwork: Twice a Year

An adult cat usually sees the vet once a year. A senior cat should go twice a year, because the conditions that affect older cats — kidney disease, thyroid problems, diabetes, dental disease — develop slowly and are far easier to manage when caught early. A senior check-up usually includes a physical examination, a weight check, blood tests, a urine test, and a blood pressure check. Bloodwork is the key part: it shows how the kidneys, liver and thyroid are working before any symptoms appear, and it gives your vet a baseline to compare against at the next visit. Keep a simple note of your cat's weight at each visit — slow weight loss is one of the earliest signs that something is changing, and it is easy to miss at home because it happens gradually. If your cat has not seen a vet in over a year, book a senior check-up now rather than waiting for a problem to appear. For general health guidance, see our Health & Tips section.

Kidney and Thyroid Warning Signs

Two conditions affect a large share of senior cats, and both start with subtle signs. Chronic kidney disease is common in older cats. The early signs are drinking more water than usual, urinating more often or in larger amounts, a reduced appetite, and gradual weight loss. Because cats naturally drink more in hot weather, the change is easy to dismiss — the clue is the trend over weeks, not a single day. Hyperthyroidism, an overactive thyroid, is also common in cats over ten. Its signs are almost the opposite: a big appetite with weight loss anyway, restlessness, a faster heartbeat, and sometimes vomiting or a scruffy coat. A cat that eats well and still loses weight should always be checked. Both conditions are manageable when found early — kidney disease with diet and hydration support, thyroid disease with medication — but both get harder to treat the longer they go unnoticed. The practical rule is simple: any lasting change in drinking, eating, weight, or energy in a senior cat is worth a vet visit, even if the cat seems fine otherwise.

Diet and Hydration: Small Changes, Big Difference

Senior cats do not all need the same food. Some need a diet that is easier on the kidneys, some need more calories because they are losing weight, and some need fewer because they move less. Do not switch your cat's food on a guess — ask your vet what fits your cat's bloodwork and weight, and then change foods gradually over a week or two, mixing the new food with the old. What matters most is that your cat keeps eating: a senior cat that stops eating, even for a day or two, can develop liver problems quickly, so a skipped meal in an older cat is a reason to call the vet, not to wait and see. Hydration matters just as much. Many senior cats do not drink enough, which makes kidney problems worse. Encourage drinking with fresh water in several places around the house, a wide shallow bowl that does not touch the whiskers, and a pet water fountain if your cat likes moving water. Wet food also helps, because it adds water to every meal. If your cat suddenly drinks much more than usual, that is a warning sign worth a vet visit, as covered above. Joint Comfort and Mobility

Arthritis is common in senior cats, and it is often missed because cats hide pain by moving less rather than by limping. The signs are subtle: hesitating before a jump, using the stairs less, sleeping in lower places, grooming less, or becoming grumpy when picked up. Make the house easier to navigate. Add a step or a sturdy box beside the bed and the sofa so your cat does not have to jump. Keep food, water and the litter box on the same floor your cat sleeps on. Put a soft, warm bed in a draught-free spot — older joints stiffen in the cold. Never give your cat human painkillers: common painkillers for people, including ibuprofen and paracetamol, are toxic to cats. If your cat seems stiff or sore, ask your vet about safe options; there are veterinary pain relief and joint supplements that can make a real difference, and your vet can advise which fits your cat.

Litter-Box Access: Low Sides, More Boxes

Litter-box problems are one of the most common senior cat complaints, and they are usually a design problem, not a behaviour problem. An older cat with stiff joints struggles to climb into a high-sided box, and a cat with kidney disease that urinates more often needs a box that is always clean and always nearby. The fixes are simple. Use a box with a low entry side, or cut a doorway into one side of a regular box. Keep one more box than the number of cats in the house, and put boxes on every floor your cat uses. Scoop daily and change the litter fully on a regular schedule — senior cats are fussier about a dirty box than younger ones. Place a box near your cat's favourite sleeping spot, so an older cat does not have to cross the whole house at night. If a previously clean cat starts going outside the box, that is a medical sign first and a behaviour problem second: urinary infections, kidney disease and arthritis all cause it, so book a vet visit before you change the litter or the box.

Grooming and Coat Care

Senior cats often groom less, especially if their joints hurt or they have dental pain, and a neglected coat mats quickly. Brush your cat gently two or three times a week — it removes loose fur, prevents mats, and gives you a chance to check the skin for lumps, sores or parasites. Check the claws too: older cats scratch less, so claws grow long and can curl into the paw pads. Trim them carefully, or ask your vet or a groomer to do it. Look inside the ears and at the teeth while you are there. Dental disease is very common in senior cats and causes real pain, which shows up as dropping food, chewing on one side, or eating less. A vet dental check is part of the senior visit for a reason. Finally, keep the coat clean without frequent baths, which most cats hate and which strip natural oils; a damp cloth wipe is usually enough for a senior cat.

Keeping Routines Stable

Senior cats cope best when life stays predictable. Keep feeding times, sleeping spots and daily routines the same, and introduce changes slowly — a new piece of furniture, a house guest, a holiday — one at a time, with a quiet retreat space your cat can always reach. Older cats often become more anxious, and some show signs of confusion at night: wandering, crying, or forgetting where the litter box is. This can be a normal part of ageing, but it can also be a medical problem, so mention it at the next vet visit rather than assuming it is just old age. Spend a little extra time with your senior cat each day: gentle play, a brush, a lap to sit on. Older cats still need stimulation, but in shorter, calmer sessions than a young cat. The goal is not to keep your cat young. It is to make the later years comfortable, predictable and free of pain — and that is mostly a matter of noticing small changes early and acting on them.

The Bottom Line

Senior cat care is mostly attention: twice-yearly vet visits with bloodwork, watching for kidney and thyroid warning signs, a diet and hydration routine that fits your cat, easy access to food, water and litter, gentle grooming, and a stable, predictable home. None of it is complicated, and none of it needs expensive equipment. What it needs is a habit of noticing — weight, drinking, appetite, movement, mood — and a vet visit whenever the trend changes. Your cat has given you eleven years or more of company. A little extra care now is how you give those years back.

Frequently asked questions

At what age is a cat considered senior?

Most vets consider a cat senior from around 11 years old, with cats over 15 often called geriatric. The exact age matters less than the changes: slower movement, more sleep, weight changes and new health issues are the real markers, and they are the reason senior cats need twice-yearly vet visits.

How often should a senior cat see the vet?

Twice a year is the usual recommendation for cats over 11. Each visit should include a physical examination, a weight check, blood tests, a urine test and a blood pressure check, so problems like kidney disease and thyroid disease are caught before symptoms appear.

What are the first signs of kidney disease in older cats?

The earliest signs are drinking more water than usual, urinating more often or in larger amounts, a reduced appetite and gradual weight loss. Because these changes develop slowly, the trend over weeks matters more than any single day — and bloodwork can confirm the problem before the signs are obvious.

Should I change my senior cat's food?

Not without asking your vet. Senior cats have different needs — some need kidney-friendly food, some need more calories, some need fewer — and the right choice depends on your cat's bloodwork and weight. Whatever you choose, change foods gradually over a week or two, and call your vet promptly if your cat stops eating.
