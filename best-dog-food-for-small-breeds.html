Best Dog Food for Small Breeds: A Practical Guide

November 28, 2026 • 18 min read • PawSmartCentral

A small dog is not simply a large dog made smaller. A Chihuahua, a Yorkshire Terrier, a Pomeranian, a Shih Tzu, a Dachshund, or a Toy Poodle eats less in total, and yet needs more energy for every kilogram of its body than a large dog does. It has a small stomach that fills quickly, a small mouth in which teeth are crowded together, and a body in which a few extra grams of food a day — an amount so small it barely registers in a measuring cup — can, over weeks, become visible extra weight. Choosing food for a small breed is therefore a different job from choosing food in general. It is about small, measured portions rather than generous bowls, about a kibble the dog can actually pick up and chew, about teeth that need regular attention, about an appetite that may be enthusiastic one week and fussy the next, and about a weight that must be watched with a scale and with hands, because the eye alone cannot judge a few dozen grams on a two-kilogram dog. This guide explains how those facts shape the choice, how to read a label with a small breed in mind, how feeding changes from puppyhood through adulthood into the senior years, and when a question about food is really a question for a vet. It is general information only. It does not diagnose a dog, it does not prescribe a diet for an individual animal, and it does not replace a vet who knows the dog. Where a health, growth, or weight question is discussed, the advice in this guide is to ask a vet.

Quick answer: There is no single best dog food for small breeds. The best choice for a particular dog is a complete and balanced food made for the dog's life stage — small-breed puppy food while the dog is growing, an adult small-breed food once growth is complete, and a senior food when age and condition call for it — in a form the dog can eat comfortably, chosen with a vet's advice, fed in weighed portions, and adjusted by body condition rather than by appetite or by the look of the bowl. The label matters, but the feeding matters more: the same good food, fed too generously, will still produce an overweight small dog, and the same ordinary food, fed carefully to a lean, active small dog, will usually serve that dog better than an expensive food fed without measure. Start with life stage, check that the food is complete and balanced, choose a kibble size and form the dog can manage, and then manage the portion, the treats, and the transition with the same care as the purchase. A small dog's ration is small, and small errors in it are large errors in proportion, which is why weighing the food, rather than estimating it, is the single most useful habit in this guide.

It helps to be clear about what "small breed" means in this guide, because the label on a bag uses the phrase loosely, and because the range it covers is wide. In general use, a small breed is a dog whose healthy adult weight is roughly below ten kilograms, and a toy breed is a dog whose healthy adult weight is very much lower again — in the region of two to four kilograms, for breeds such as the Chihuahua, the Yorkshire Terrier, and the Toy Poodle. A dog that sits near the boundary, such as a small Cocker Spaniel or a large Dachshund, should be fed by its own adult size, growth pattern, and vet's advice rather than by a label category. What matters is not the word on the bag but the facts behind it: how much the dog will weigh as an adult, how much energy it uses, how small its stomach and mouth are, whether its teeth are crowded, and whether it has any condition that affects its diet. A food described as "for small breeds" is useful only if its formulation and its kibble actually reflect those facts, and the sections below explain how to judge that from the label and from the dog in front of you, rather than from the photograph on the front of the bag.

This guide covers the small-breed angle only. It does not repeat the detailed advice in our other food guides, which are linked where they are relevant: our guide to the best dog food for puppies covers the first months of feeding in detail; our guide to best dog food for senior dogs covers the changes that come with age; our guide to the best dog food for large breeds covers the opposite end of the size range, and is useful for households that keep dogs of very different sizes; our guide to best dog foods for allergies covers food allergy and intolerance; our guide to best dog food for sensitive stomachs covers digestive upset; our guide to best cat food for kittens is the small-animal companion to this guide for kitten owners; and our guide to how much food to feed a dog covers portion size and body condition in full. Use those guides for their subjects, and use this guide for the question they do not answer on their own: what, if anything, should be different because the dog is small.

1. Why Size Changes the Feeding Question

Four differences make small-breed feeding its own subject, and each of them is practical rather than theoretical. The first is the rate at which a small dog uses energy. A small dog loses heat faster, relative to its size, than a large dog, and it commonly uses more energy for every kilogram of body weight, which means that its food must supply more energy per bite, and that a small dog that misses meals, or that eats too little, can run short of energy sooner than a large dog would. That does not mean a small dog should be fed without limit. It means that the margin between enough and too much is narrow, and that the portion must be right, in grams, rather than approximately right, by eye. A large dog's ration can be a little wrong and the error is absorbed. A small dog's ration, wrong by the same proportion, is wrong by a small number of grams that is easy to add by accident — a heaped scoop, a handful of treats, a scrap from a plate — and the dog's weight will show it within weeks.

The second difference is the size of the stomach. A small dog's stomach is small, and it fills quickly, which means that a small dog cannot comfortably take in a whole day's food in one large meal, and that a meal which is too large, or which is eaten too fast, is more likely to be refused, vomited, or left unfinished. Small dogs, and particularly small puppies, therefore do better on several small meals a day than on one large one, and the meal pattern should be regular, calm, and predictable, with fresh water available at all times. A small dog that is fed once a day, in a large bowl, and that leaves food, picks at it, or vomits after eating, may not be fussy. It may be telling the owner, in the only way it can, that the meal is too large, too fast, or too infrequent, and the response should be to change the pattern — smaller meals, more often, measured — before changing the food. If the pattern is corrected and the problem persists, the dog should be seen by a vet, because persistent vomiting, refusal to eat, or weight loss in a small dog should never be dismissed as fussiness.

The third difference is the mouth and the teeth. Small breeds commonly have the same number of teeth as large breeds, fitted into a much smaller jaw, which means the teeth are crowded, the gaps between them are narrow, and tartar and gum problems develop more readily. Food interacts with that anatomy in two ways: a kibble that is too large to be picked up and chewed comfortably will be refused, swallowed whole, or dropped, and a diet that is entirely soft, with no chewing at all, does nothing to exercise the teeth and gums, though chewing alone does not prevent dental disease. The practical conclusion is to choose a form the dog can eat comfortably — a small kibble, or a wet food, or a mixture, as the dog prefers and the vet advises — and to treat dental care as a separate routine, of brushing, dental checks, and professional cleaning where a vet advises it, rather than as something a food alone will provide. A food that claims to clean teeth, or to prevent dental disease, should be read with the same caution as any other health claim on a bag, and a dog with dental pain, bad breath, drooling, or difficulty eating needs a vet, not a new bag of food.

The fourth difference is how easily extra weight hides, and how much it matters. A kilogram of extra weight on a thirty-kilogram dog is a small proportion. A kilogram of extra weight on a three-kilogram dog is a third of the dog's body weight, and it is a load on joints, on breathing, and on general health that is out of all proportion to its size. Extra weight on a small dog is also easy to miss, because the dog is picked up, carried, dressed, and viewed from above, rather than weighed, and because a small dog that gains weight gradually still looks, to a familiar eye, like the same small dog. The discipline that prevents it is the same as for a large dog, applied with more precision: weigh the food in grams, weigh the dog regularly on scales that can register small changes, look at the dog's shape with hands as well as eyes, and keep a short note of weight and portion so that a slow drift is visible before it becomes a problem. The rest of this guide is detail around that discipline, adapted to the small scale on which it must be practised.

2. Small Puppies: Frequent Meals, Steady Growth, and Early Advice

A small-breed puppy grows quickly, reaches its adult size sooner than a large breed, and has very little reserve if it misses meals or eats too little. That combination makes the puppy period the period in which feeding routine matters most, and in which a vet's early advice is most valuable. A very small puppy should eat several small meals a day — commonly three or four, and sometimes more for a very tiny puppy, on a vet's or breeder's advice — at regular times, with the portion weighed, and with the puppy watched while it eats, so that the owner knows how much was actually eaten, rather than how much was put down. A puppy that eats eagerly, gains weight steadily, and is alert and active is usually thriving. A puppy that is sleepy, shaky, weak, or unwilling to eat, or that misses meals, should be seen by a vet promptly, and should not be left to "eat when it is hungry", because a very small puppy's reserves are small, and the signs of low energy can develop quickly. This guide does not diagnose those signs, and it does not present any feeding change as a treatment for them. It states the practical rule: a small puppy that is not eating, or that seems unwell, is a vet visit, and the visit should not wait for the next routine appointment.

Growth in a small breed should be steady, and the food should be formulated for growth, in a version that takes small breeds into account where one is available, because the balance of nutrients that supports growth in a small puppy differs from the balance that suits an adult dog, and the kibble should be small enough for a small mouth to manage. Adding supplements, extra calcium, or extra vitamins to a complete puppy food is a poor idea unless a vet has advised it for a specific reason, for the same reason it is a poor idea for a large puppy: a complete food already contains those nutrients in a designed balance, and adding more of one part of the balance can disturb the whole. An owner who wants to help a small puppy grow well should resist the temptation to improve a complete food with extras, and should instead spend the same attention on the portion, the feeding routine, the weaning and transition history the breeder provides, and the vet's growth checks. The breeder's information matters here: what the puppy was eating, how often, and how much, before it came home, is the starting point for the feeding at home, and a change from that food, if one is to be made, should be made gradually, over a week or more, mixing the old and new foods in changing proportions, so that the digestive system adjusts. A sudden change of food, at any age, is a common cause of digestive upset, and a puppy whose food is changed suddenly and whose stools loosen is often reacting to the change itself rather than to the new food.

The age at which a small-breed puppy moves to adult food is another decision that should be made with a vet rather than by the calendar on the bag alone. Bag guidance gives a general age range, but breeds differ, and individual dogs differ within a breed, and a small breed commonly reaches adult size sooner than a large breed, so the switch commonly comes sooner, but not on a fixed date that suits every dog. A common pattern is to keep the puppy on its growth food until growth is substantially complete, and to make the change gradually, as before, judging the new food over weeks, not over a single meal. If a new food is to be tried, change it slowly, keep the portion weighed, and watch the stools, the appetite, the coat, and the weight, recording what changes and when. For the detail of the early months, see our puppy food guide, and for the questions to raise at a puppy check, see the vet-questions section near the end of this guide. The puppy period is short, the habits formed in it are long-lasting, and the effort spent on routine, measurement, and vet advice in the first months will repay itself for the whole of the dog's life.

3. Reading the Label With a Small Breed in Mind

A dog food label is a legal and practical document, and it rewards a slow reading. The front of the bag is advertising. The back of the bag is information, and the information that matters for a small breed is the same information that matters for any dog, read with small-breed questions in mind. Start with the statement that the food is complete and balanced, and with the life stage it is made for — growth, adult maintenance, all life stages, or senior — because a food that is not complete, or that is made for a different life stage, is not a suitable everyday diet however attractive the front of the bag looks. A food described as "complementary" is meant to be fed alongside something else, and feeding it alone, as the whole diet, will leave gaps. A food made for all life stages should state that it meets the needs of growth, and it should be judged, for a small puppy, by whether the kibble size, the feeding guide, and the formulation suit a small mouth and a small stomach. If any of those points is unclear from the label, ask a vet before using the food for a growing small breed, rather than assuming the claim on the front covers it.

Next, read the feeding guide, and treat it as a starting point rather than a prescription, and read it in grams. Feeding guides are written for an average dog of a given weight, and no individual dog is exactly average. An active small dog needs more than a guide written for a quiet dog of the same weight. A neutered dog kept indoors needs less. A dog in a cold climate, a dog recovering from illness, and a dog that is already overweight each need a different amount from the guide figure. The guide is therefore the place to begin, and body condition is the place to adjust from, as the feeding-amounts guide explains in detail. Weigh the food, in grams, on kitchen scales, every day, at least until the measure becomes familiar, because a cup measure of kibble varies with the size and shape of the kibble, and a small dog's ration measured by eye can drift by a substantial proportion without the owner noticing. A kitchen scale, used daily, is one of the cheapest and most useful tools in small-breed feeding, and it is more important for a small dog than for a large one, for the simple reason that the ration is smaller and the error is proportionally larger. A scoop, a cup, and a handful are not measures. Grams are.

The ingredient list deserves a realistic reading, and the same cautions apply as for any dog food. Ingredients are listed in order of weight before cooking, which means that a named meat listed first may contribute less of the finished food than its position suggests, once water is removed, and that a list that begins with several grains may contain more grain, in total, than a list that begins with a meat followed by one grain. That does not make grains bad, and it does not make a meat-first list automatically good. What it means is that the ingredient list should not be read as a ranking of quality, and that single-ingredient claims on the front of the bag should not be mistaken for the whole recipe. The more useful questions to ask of a label are practical: is the food complete and balanced for the dog's life stage, does it state a feeding guide in grams, does it name a manufacturer and give contact details, does it state the kibble size or form, and does it carry a batch or best-before marking that suggests proper quality control. A food that answers those questions clearly is a food that can be judged. A food that hides behind vague wording and dramatic claims is a food that cannot be judged, and should not be chosen on the strength of its advertising.

Two label claims deserve particular caution for small breeds. The first is any claim that a food prevents, treats, or cures a health condition, including a dental, joint, or weight condition. A complete food can support general health, and a vet may recommend a specific therapeutic diet for a diagnosed condition, but an ordinary retail food that claims to treat a disease should be treated with scepticism, and any dog with a diagnosed condition should be fed on a vet's advice, not on a bag's promise. The second is the claim that a food is suitable for every dog, at every age, in every circumstance, including toy breeds, large breeds, puppies, and seniors, in a single formulation. Small-breed puppies, pregnant dogs, senior dogs, and dogs with health problems have different needs, and a single food that claims to meet all of them equally well is claiming more than a label can prove. Choose a food for the dog in front of you, at its current stage, and be prepared to change it, gradually and with a vet's advice, when the stage changes. The same caution applies to foods marketed with a breed name on the bag: a food named for a breed is not necessarily formulated for that breed's particular needs, and the label, the life stage, and the feeding guide are better evidence than the breed in the brand name.

4. Portions, Treats, and Body Condition: Where Small-Breed Feeding Is Won or Lost

If this guide had to be reduced to one habit, it would be this: weigh the food, count the treats, and check the body. Everything else — the brand, the formulation, the flavour — matters less than those three actions repeated daily, because a small dog's weight is decided by the total amount eaten, not by the quality of any single meal, and because the total is small enough that uncounted extras dominate it. The method is straightforward. Weigh the daily ration in grams, split it into the number of meals the dog's age and routine require, and feed that amount and no more, except for adjustments a vet has advised. Count treats, table scraps, chews, dental chews, and the food used for training as part of the day's total, not as extras on top of it. A training session that uses a handful of treats, repeated daily, can add a substantial proportion to a small dog's intake, and the owner who counts it will adjust the meals down to make room for it, while the owner who does not count it will wonder why the dog is gaining weight on a "correct" ration. Use small treats for training, break larger treats into small pieces, use part of the weighed ration as training rewards where that works, and keep a single place — a jar, a tin, a measured scoop — from which all the day's extras come, so that everyone in the household is working from the same total. A small dog fed by a household, rather than by one person, needs a household agreement, written down, until it becomes habit, because a dog fed a little by each of three people is fed three times over.

Body condition is the check that turns a weighed ration into a correct ration, and it is a hands-on check, not a visual guess, and it is harder to judge by eye on a small, fluffy, or long-coated dog than on a short-coated large dog. Stand the dog up, run your hands over its ribs, and look at its shape from above and from the side, parting the coat where necessary so that the shape, not the fur, is what is judged. You should be able to feel the ribs easily under a thin covering, without pressing hard, and you should be able to see a waist — a narrowing behind the ribs when seen from above, and a tuck of the belly when seen from the side. If the ribs are hard to feel, or the waist has disappeared, the dog is carrying more weight than it should, and the ration, the treats, or both need to come down, gradually, with the change monitored over weeks. If the ribs are prominent, the spine and hip bones are easily felt, or the dog has lost weight without a change in feeding, the dog should be seen by a vet, because weight loss in a small dog can be a sign of a health problem as well as of underfeeding, and a small dog has little reserve to lose. Weigh the dog regularly, on scales that register small changes — a kitchen scale for a very small dog, or the same bathroom scales with the owner holding the dog and subtracting, used consistently — and keep a note of the weight and the date, because a slow gain of a hundred grams a month is invisible to the eye and obvious in a notebook. Our feeding-amounts guide explains body-condition scoring and portion adjustment step by step, and it is the companion to this section.

Meal frequency also matters, and it changes with age. Small puppies eat several small meals a day, because their stomachs are small and their energy needs are high relative to their size. Adult small dogs commonly eat two meals a day, morning and evening, which suits both digestion and routine, and some small adults do better on three smaller meals, particularly if they are prone to going too long between meals, to vomiting bile on an empty stomach, or to seeming unwell when meals are spaced widely. A single very large meal a day is not ideal for a small breed, for two reasons: it is a large volume for a small stomach to take in at once, and it concentrates the day's eating into a single event, which makes portion control harder and leaves a long gap in which the dog is hungry and the household is tempted to fill the gap with extras. Whatever the frequency, keep the routine steady: the same times, the same place, the same weighed amount, with fresh water available at all times. Small dogs drink a good deal of water relative to their size, and a dog that is fed a dry food needs ready access to water throughout the day. A sudden change in drinking — much more, or much less — is worth mentioning to a vet, as it can be a sign of a health problem, and it should not be dismissed as a quirk. The same applies to a sudden change in appetite, in either direction, in a dog whose routine has not changed.

Exercise and food belong together, and they should be adjusted together, with the adjustment following the dog's weight and condition rather than the owner's guess. A small dog that becomes more active — longer walks, a new sport, a move to a house with a garden — will need more food, and a small dog that becomes less active — an injury, a quieter routine, a move to a flat, a period of bad weather — will need less. The adjustment should be gradual, and it should be judged by the notebook, not by the owner's impression of how much exercise the dog "must" be getting. A small dog that is carried for much of the day, that travels in a bag or a carrier, or that spends most of its time indoors, may be getting very little exercise however busy its owner's day appears, and its ration should reflect that. A dog recovering from injury is a special case: it needs enough food to heal, but it cannot burn off extra food, and it is easy, during a recovery, to feed generously out of sympathy and to produce a dog that is both injured and overweight when the injury heals. Ask the vet who is treating the injury about feeding during recovery, and follow that advice rather than the general guidance in this guide. The same applies in reverse: a dog that is losing weight on a ration that used to maintain it, or a dog whose appetite has changed, should be checked before the ration is changed, because the change may be a symptom rather than a feeding problem.

5. Teeth, Chewing, and the Form of the Food

Teeth deserve a section of their own for small breeds, because dental problems are common, because they affect eating directly, and because the choice of food form — dry, wet, or mixed — is often made, by owners, as if it were a dental decision, when it is really a preference, convenience, and comfort decision, with dental care as a separate matter. A small dog's crowded teeth trap food and plaque more readily than a large dog's spaced teeth, and the gums are more readily irritated, which means that dental disease can progress quietly, without the owner noticing, until the dog is in pain, eats on one side, drops food, or refuses hard food altogether. The signs worth acting on are bad breath that is new or worsening, drooling, pawing at the mouth, difficulty picking up or chewing food, bleeding gums, loose teeth, and a reluctance to have the mouth touched. Any of those signs is a reason to see a vet, and the vet, not a food change, should decide whether the dog needs a dental examination, a cleaning, an extraction, or a change of diet. A dog that suddenly refuses a food it previously ate, or that eats only soft food, may be in dental pain, and the owner who responds by buying a succession of softer foods, without a dental check, may be delaying the treatment the dog needs.

The form of the food should follow the dog, the household, and a vet's advice, in that order. Dry food is convenient, measurable in grams, and commonly the most economical way to feed a small dog, and a small kibble is easier for a small mouth to pick up and chew than a large one. Wet food is more palatable to many small dogs, contains more water, and can be useful for a dog that drinks little, for a dog with dental problems, or for a dog whose appetite needs encouragement, but it needs to be measured just as carefully as dry food, because a pouch or a tin is not a portion in itself, and it spoils once opened, so it must be refrigerated and used within the period the manufacturer states. A mixture of wet and dry, with the total weighed, suits many small dogs, provided the mixture is consistent and the total is counted, and provided the dog's teeth and stools are watched when the mixture changes. The form matters less than the completeness of the diet and the accuracy of the portion, and a household that feeds dry food carefully is feeding better than a household that feeds wet food generously without measure. Whatever the form, two practical habits apply: keep dry food in its original bag or in a clean, sealed container, in a cool, dry place, respecting the best-before date, and wash the bowls daily, because a small dog's bowls are used heavily relative to their size and are a place where bacteria can multiply.

Chews, dental chews, and bones need a note of caution for small breeds, because the size that is safe for a large dog is not safe for a small one, and because a chew that is too hard, too large, or that splinters can damage teeth or cause choking. Choose chews sized for small dogs, supervise chewing, remove a chew that has broken into pieces, and count every chew, including a dental chew, as part of the day's food total, because dental chews contain energy, and a daily dental chew, uncounted, is a daily addition to a small ration. Do not give cooked bones, which can splinter, and do not give a small dog access to a large dog's chews or food, in a multi-dog household, without supervision, because the small dog may take food that is too large, too hard, or too much for it, and because the large dog may guard its food. Ask a vet which chews, if any, suit the individual dog, particularly if the dog has dental problems, a sensitive stomach, or a history of swallowing pieces whole. A chew is a supplement to a diet and to dental care, not a substitute for either, and a dog that has dental disease needs treatment, not a chew marketed as a treatment.

6. Picky Eaters, Toppers, and Table Scraps

Picky eating is the complaint most often made about small breeds, and it is a complaint that needs careful handling, because the label "picky" covers several different situations, and each needs a different response. The first situation is a dog that is genuinely unwell, in pain, or frightened, and that will not eat for that reason. The response is a vet visit, not a new food. The second is a dog that has learned that refusing food produces something better — a topper, a different food, a scrap from the table, a hand-fed morsel — and that therefore refuses with increasing confidence, because the strategy works. The response is consistency: a weighed meal, put down for a short, fixed time, removed if uneaten, and offered again at the next mealtime, with no topping, no alternative, and no scrap in between, until the dog eats its measured meals reliably. That routine feels harsh to an owner, and it should not be applied to a puppy, to a dog that is unwell, to a dog that has not eaten for a concerning period, or without a vet's advice where the dog's health is in question, but for a healthy adult dog that has learned to hold out, it is the routine that works, and it commonly works within days. The third situation is a dog whose food genuinely does not suit it — a kibble too large, a flavour it dislikes, a food that causes digestive upset — and the response is a slow change to a different food, judged over weeks, with the stools, the appetite, and the weight recorded. The difficulty is telling the second situation from the third, and the way to tell them apart is to change one thing at a time, slowly, and to record the result, rather than changing foods, toppers, and routines all at once.

Toppers — a spoonful of wet food, a little warmed water, a small amount of a different food mixed in — are not wrong in themselves, but they should be counted, consistent, and small. A topper that changes every meal, that grows larger each week, or that is added because the dog refused the plain food, teaches the dog to wait for the topper, and it adds uncounted energy to a small ration. A topper that is the same every meal, that is a small, weighed part of the ration, and that is mixed in from the start, is a part of the diet, not a negotiation, and it can help a dog that eats reluctantly to eat its measured amount. Table scraps should be treated with the same discipline, and with more caution, because human food is commonly salted, fatty, or seasoned in ways that do not suit a dog, and because some human foods are harmful to dogs. A scrap given occasionally, in a tiny amount, from a safe food, and counted as part of the day's total, is a different matter from a regular share of the family's meals, and the owner who is unsure whether a food is safe for a dog should ask a vet or check a reliable source before giving it, rather than giving it and hoping. A small dog's stomach leaves very little room for error, and a scrap that is a trivial amount for a person is a substantial amount for a two-kilogram dog. Children in the household should be included in the agreement about scraps, because a child who feeds the dog from the table, with love and without measure, can undo a weighed ration in a single meal.

Hand-feeding deserves a brief note, because it is common with small dogs, and because it is often a symptom rather than a solution. A dog that will eat only from a hand, or only if coaxed, has usually learned that coaxing produces attention, and the attention, not the food, is the reward. Hand-feeding a small amount, as part of training, or to encourage a puppy, a convalescent dog, or a dog that is genuinely frightened, is a different matter, and it should be done on a vet's advice where the dog is unwell. As a routine, hand-feeding teaches the dog that food comes from the hand, not from the bowl, and it makes the feeding routine dependent on the owner's presence and patience, which is a fragile arrangement for a dog that will be fed by other people, in kennels, or in a new home. The bowl, the weighed portion, the fixed time, and the removal of uneaten food are the routine that survives a change of feeder, and they are the routine worth establishing, however much the owner enjoys the closeness of hand-feeding. If a dog will not eat from a bowl at all, that is a question for a vet, not a reason to hand-feed indefinitely, because the refusal may be pain, fear, or illness, and the hand-feeding may be masking it.

7. Changing Foods, Allergies, and Sensitive Stomachs

Changing a small dog's food is a common need and a common source of avoidable upset, and the method is the same as for any dog, applied with more care because the amounts are smaller and the stomach is smaller. Mix the new food with the old, in proportions that shift gradually over seven to ten days, watch the stools and the appetite, and keep the portion weighed throughout, because a change of food is also commonly a change of energy density, and the same volume of a new food may contain more or less energy than the same volume of the old. If the stools loosen, slow the change down, go back a step, and proceed more slowly. If the upset persists, if the dog stops eating, vomits repeatedly, or seems unwell, stop the change and ask a vet, rather than pressing on with a food that may not suit the dog. A food trial, where a vet is investigating a possible food-related problem, is different: it follows the vet's instructions exactly, including the exclusion of treats and extras, and it should not be improvised by the owner, because a trial that is not followed strictly gives an answer that cannot be trusted, and because a small dog on a trial has very little margin for an error in the trial itself.

Allergies and sensitive stomachs are the subjects of two of our other guides, and they are mentioned here only to place them correctly in a small-breed decision. A food allergy in a dog is an immune response to a particular ingredient, and it is diagnosed by a vet, commonly by an exclusion diet, not by a change of brand bought on a guess. A sensitive stomach is a description of a pattern — loose stools, wind, or vomiting that recurs — and it has many possible causes, of which food is only one. In both cases, the small-breed owner should resist the temptation to solve the problem by buying a succession of special foods, each tried briefly, because that approach produces a confused record, a dog whose gut never settles, and no reliable evidence about what helps, and because each change, in a small dog, is a larger proportional disturbance than the same change in a large dog. The right course is a vet visit, a clear history of what the dog has eaten and what the symptoms have been, and a plan that is followed long enough to judge. Our guides to dog foods for allergies and to dog food for sensitive stomachs explain the diagnosis, the diet trial, and the feeding that follows, and they should be read alongside a vet's advice, not instead of it. The same caution applies to the many special diets marketed for small breeds, for teeth, for coats, for digestion, for weight, or for "sensitive" dogs: each may suit some dogs, and each is marketed in language that suggests it suits all dogs with the named concern. Ask whether the dog has the concern, whether a vet has identified it, and whether the diet has been recommended for that dog, before buying.

8. Life Stages: Puppy, Adult, and Senior Small Dogs

A small breed passes through the same life stages as any dog, but the stages last different lengths, and the feeding at each stage has a different emphasis. The puppy stage is short and intense, and its emphasis is routine, measurement, and vet advice, as section 2 explains. The adult stage is the longest stage overall, and its emphasis is maintenance: a weighed ration, a stable routine, a lean body condition, and a food that suits the dog's activity and teeth. The adult stage is also the stage at which owners most often drift, because the dog looks settled, the routine feels established, and the slow gain of weight over a year or two goes unnoticed, particularly in a dog that is carried, dressed, and viewed from above rather than weighed. The defence against drift is the same discipline as before: regular weighing, regular hands-on condition checks, and a ration that is adjusted when the dog's activity or condition changes, rather than a ration that stays fixed because it was once correct. A food change in adulthood is sometimes needed — for a change in activity, a new health issue, a dental problem, or a food that no longer suits the dog — and when it is needed, it should be made gradually, with the same slow transition as any other change, and judged over weeks.

The senior stage arrives gradually, and its signs are often clearer in behaviour than in age alone: a dog that sleeps more, that is stiffer after rest, that is less keen on walks, that loses muscle, that gains weight on a ration that once maintained it, or that has dental problems that make eating difficult. Senior small dogs may need a food with a different balance — commonly a food that supports lean muscle, that is easier to digest and to chew, and that reflects a lower energy need — but the decision should follow the individual dog, not the birthday. Some senior dogs remain active and lean on their adult food for years. Others need a change much earlier, particularly where teeth, joints, or digestion are concerned. A vet who examines the senior dog, checks its weight, its teeth, its joints, and its blood work where appropriate, is the right person to advise on the change, and the change should be made gradually, as before. For the detail of senior feeding, appetite changes, and the checks worth discussing at a senior visit, see our senior dog food guide, which covers that stage in full and is not repeated here. A senior small dog should be weighed more often, not less, because weight loss in a senior dog can be a sign of a health problem, and because a small senior dog has little reserve.

Two transitions deserve a note of their own, because they are the points at which feeding most often goes wrong. The first is neutering. Neutering commonly reduces a dog's energy needs, and a dog that is fed the same ration after neutering as before will commonly gain weight. The adjustment should be made promptly after neutering, on a vet's advice, by reducing the ration and monitoring condition, rather than waiting for the weight gain to appear and then dieting the dog. The second is a change of home or routine — a move, a new household member, a change in who feeds the dog, a period of kennelling or holiday care, a new baby, or a new dog in the household. Each of these can change how much the dog is fed, by whom, and with what extras, and each is a point at which the household should agree the ration, the treats, the measurer, and the scrap rule, so that a dog fed by several people is not fed several times over. A small dog that is fed by a household, rather than by one person, needs a household agreement, and the agreement should be written down, on the fridge or in a note, until it becomes habit. A small dog that moves to a new home should keep its previous food and routine at first, changing only gradually, once it has settled, because the stress of a move is enough without a change of diet on top of it.

9. Myths That Cost Small Dogs Their Health

Several myths about small-breed feeding circulate widely, and each of them leads to a concrete mistake. The first is that a small dog needs very little food, and therefore the amount does not matter, and can be estimated. The amount is small, and therefore the amount matters more, not less, because a small error is a large proportion of the ration. The second myth is that a small dog is fragile and should be fed generously, or fed human food, to "build it up". A small dog that is fed generously becomes an overweight small dog, with the joint, breathing, and dental consequences that follow, and a small dog fed human food as a routine is fed a diet that is not balanced for a dog, however lovingly it is given. The third myth is that a small dog that is thin must be underfed, or that a small dog that looks "well covered" is healthy. Body condition, judged by hands and by a vet, is the measure, not the eye's habit of expecting a small dog to look round, or to look delicate. A lean small dog, with ribs easily felt and a visible waist, is a healthy small dog, and a small dog that looks round is commonly an overweight small dog, however appealing the roundness appears.

The fourth myth is that expensive food is better food, and that a cheap food must be poor, or that a food named for a breed, or sold in a small, pretty bag, must be formulated for that breed. Price reflects many things — branding, distribution, packaging, and the cost of the ingredients — and it is not a reliable measure of nutritional quality. A complete and balanced food from a reputable manufacturer, at a moderate price, fed in a weighed portion, will serve most small dogs well. An expensive food, fed without measure, will not. The fifth myth is that a dog that has eaten a food for years without obvious trouble must be thriving on it. Dogs are stoic, and the effects of a diet that is adequate but not ideal, or of a slow weight gain, accumulate quietly, and a small dog's weight gain accumulates faster, in proportion, than a large dog's. A dog that is fed the same food for years should still be weighed, still be condition-checked, still have its teeth checked, and still be examined by a vet, because "no obvious trouble" is a low standard for a diet that shapes the dog's whole life. The sixth myth, and the most expensive, is that a supplement, a powder, or an oil added to a complete food will fix a problem that the food itself has not caused. Supplements have a place, on a vet's advice, for a specific dog with a specific need. Added without advice, they are an uncontrolled change to a balanced diet, in a dog whose ration is small and whose margin is narrow, and they delay the examination that would identify the real problem.

10. Questions to Ask Your Vet About Food

A vet visit is the point at which general guidance becomes individual advice, and it is worth arriving with questions written down, with the food's name, the portion in grams, the number of meals, the treats, and the dog's weight history, so that the vet can judge the whole picture rather than a single complaint. The questions below are a starting point, not a script, and the vet's answers, for the individual dog, should take precedence over anything in this guide.

  • Is this food right for my dog's life stage and size? Bring the bag, or a photograph of the label, including the life-stage statement, the feeding guide, and the ingredient list, and ask whether the formulation and the kibble size suit the dog.
  • How much should my dog eat, in grams, and how often? Ask for a figure in grams, for the number of meals, and for the treat allowance, and ask how the figure should change if the dog's activity changes.
  • What weight and body condition should I aim for? Ask the vet to show you, with hands on the dog, what the ribs, the waist, and the tuck should feel and look like, and how often the dog should be weighed.
  • How are the teeth, and what dental routine should I follow? Ask whether the teeth need treatment, whether the food form should change, and what brushing, chewing, or cleaning routine the vet advises.
  • When should I change foods, and how? Ask when the puppy-to-adult or adult-to-senior change should happen, how gradual the transition should be, and what signs should prompt a faster change or a vet visit.
  • Is there anything in my dog's history that changes the feeding? Ask whether a previous illness, a medication, a dental problem, or a family history affects the diet, and whether any supplement or special diet is needed, or whether it should be avoided.

Keep the vet's answers with the feeding record, with the weight notes and the portion, so that the next visit starts from what was agreed, rather than from memory. A feeding record that contains the food, the grams, the meals, the treats, the weight, and the vet's advice is a short document, and it is the document that makes every later feeding decision easier, because it replaces guessing with evidence. If the vet's advice differs from this guide, follow the vet's advice, because the vet knows the dog, and this guide does not.

11. A Practical Checklist for Choosing and Feeding

The checklist below gathers the guide into a sequence that can be followed at the shop and at home. Work through it in order, and do not skip the steps that seem obvious, because the obvious steps are the ones most often skipped in practice, and because, for a small dog, the obvious steps are the ones that matter most.

  1. Know the dog. Record the breed or mix, the age, the current weight, whether the dog is neutered, how active it is, how its teeth are, and any health issue a vet has identified. The food decision follows these facts, not the other way round.
  2. Choose the life stage. Small-breed puppy food while growing, adult food once growth is complete, senior food when the individual dog's age and condition call for it. Ask a vet when the switch should happen.
  3. Check completeness and form. Confirm the food is complete and balanced for the life stage, that the kibble size and form suit a small mouth, and that the feeding guide is stated in grams.
  4. Weigh the ration. Weigh the daily amount in grams, on kitchen scales, split it into the meals the dog's age requires, and record the figure. Do not estimate by cup, scoop, or handful.
  5. Count everything. Treats, chews, dental chews, toppers, training rewards, and scraps are part of the day's total. Keep one jar or tin for the day's extras, and agree the rule with the whole household.
  6. Check the body. Feel the ribs, look for the waist and the tuck, weigh the dog regularly, and keep a note of weight and portion. Adjust the ration by condition, gradually, and ask a vet if weight changes without a reason.
  7. Care for the teeth. Follow the dental routine a vet advises, watch for the signs of dental pain, and treat a change in eating as a reason for a dental check, not only as a reason for a new food.
  8. Change slowly. Change foods over seven to ten days, mixing old and new, watching stools and appetite, and judging the new food over weeks. Follow a vet's trial exactly, where a trial has been advised.
  9. Keep the routine. The same times, the same place, the same weighed amount, fresh water always available, and the same rule for everyone who feeds the dog.
  10. Ask the vet. Bring the food, the grams, the weight record, and the questions in section 10 to every check, and follow the individual advice given for the individual dog.

A small dog, fed well, is a dog whose size is no disadvantage. It is a dog that eats measured meals, that keeps a lean, active body, that has teeth that are cared for, that is weighed and watched, and that is fed by a household that agrees on the ration and keeps to it. The food itself is the least difficult part of that picture. The difficult part is the discipline: the scales, the notebook, the agreement, the vet visit, and the willingness to feed by grams and by condition rather than by appetite, by habit, or by affection expressed as food. Affection, for a small dog, is better expressed in attention, in play, in walks suited to the dog's size, and in the time spent with the dog, than in extra food, because extra food, in a small dog, is not a kindness. It is a burden the dog carries on small joints, with small lungs, for the whole of its life. Feed the measured amount, count the treats, check the body, care for the teeth, and ask the vet. The small dog will repay the discipline with the years of active, healthy life that careful feeding makes possible.
